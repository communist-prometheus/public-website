**_Sandcastles of Fictitious Capital: AI Infrastructure and the Material Contradictions of Capitalist Accumulation_**

# Algorithms and Capital: Material Contradictions of the Global AI Industry

**_The November slump in the technology market, which wiped billions off the capitalisation of the sector’s giants and was accompanied by the sale of bank loans for data centre construction, exposed the vulnerability of the global artificial intelligence boom. The bourgeois press reduces these events to political conjuncture or a temporary market cooling. Behind the stock-market bulletins, however, lies a fundamental question of political economy: is the AI industry capable of securing – whether through production or appropriation – a mass of surplus value sufficient to pay back its infrastructural investments, or are we witnessing the inflation of a bubble of fictitious capital? At the centre of our inquiry is the structural gap between the multi-year cycle of building gigawatt-scale facilities and the rapid moral depreciation of the technological core, which demands payback within three to five years. Through the categories of the organic composition of capital and monopoly rent, this text analyses the nature of contemporary overaccumulation. We examine how the expectation of sustained demand is propped up by mutual commitments within a narrow circle of technology corporations, the extraction of rent, and the exploitation of a globally distributed proletariat across the imperialist supply chain._**

## Introduction. A Market Slump or the Exposure of Contradictions of Accumulation?

The events of early November 2026 on US financial markets once again demonstrated the vulnerability of the artificial intelligence investment boom. According to aggregate data from analytical agencies (such as Bloomberg Intelligence) and corporate filings with the US Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC), in 2024–2025 alone the combined capital expenditure (CAPEX) of four giants – Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, and Meta – exceeded an astronomical $400 billion, the lion’s share of which went into purchasing AI accelerators and building data centres. Following the midterm elections, which strengthened the positions of opponents of unchecked data centre construction, the US stock market suffered a sharp drop.

On 5 November, the Nasdaq 100 fell by 6.4 %, and the Nasdaq Composite by 5.5 %. Market capitalisation losses among companies listed on the Nasdaq were estimated at roughly $2.5 trillion, of which around $1 trillion fell on the “Magnificent Seven” (Nvidia, Microsoft, Alphabet / Google, Amazon, Meta / Facebook, Apple, Tesla). This was the largest single-day drop in the technology market since April 2025. The main blow fell on semiconductor manufacturers and cloud infrastructure operators, though the sell-off spread to other sectors as well. At the same time, it emerged that the Dutch bank ING had put up for sale a portfolio of data centre construction loans totalling roughly $500 million. Among the twelve claims placed on auction is a $25 million loan tranche linked to one of the facilities of the Stargate project in New Mexico. The auction is being conducted under the BWIC[^1] procedure, with bids accepted until 19 November.

Selling a loan portfolio does not mean ING has written off the corresponding claims as non-performing. Yet it reveals another side of what is happening: the lending to AI infrastructure has already formed an independent market of financial claims, on which banks can redistribute risks without waiting for facilities to be completed and brought into operation. Thus, projects not yet materially realised become the object of an autonomous movement of finance capital.

The bourgeois press explains what is happening primarily through political causes: the Democratic victory in the midterm elections, pledges by new governors to restrict data centre construction, and public discontent over rising electricity tariffs and water consumption. These circumstances do indeed affect investor expectations. Yet they do not explain the underlying causes of the instability that has emerged and, above all, do not answer the decisive question: does AI create enough surplus value to pay for the infrastructure built for it?

A political shock may pull the trigger, but it does not load the gun.

Marxist analysis requires examining financial fluctuations not in isolation, but in connection with the process of capitalist reproduction. The question is not merely how justified the stock-market valuations of technology corporations are, but what contradictions arise when vast volumes of social capital are converted into infrastructure whose profitability depends on as yet unrealised expectations. Demand for computation is derived: it arises in the first instance not for the sake of computation itself, but for the sake of what computation allows one to save or appropriate. It is therefore necessary to establish three things in sequence: what share of the AI industry’s revenues rests on new value created by productive labour, and what share represents a redistribution of already produced surplus value and the appropriation of monopoly rent; how expected revenues are transformed into credit obligations and financial titles; and finally, whether real cash flows over the economic life of the equipment are capable of recouping the advanced capital, covering financing costs, and securing a normal profit. Only then can one judge to what extent the market capitalisation of AI companies reflects real possibilities of accumulation, and to what extent it represents an expansion of fictitious capital.

The artificial intelligence boom exposes the basic contradiction of capitalism – the conflict between the social character of production and the private form of appropriation. By itself, however, that statement is insufficient: any mature branch of industry fits it. The specificity of the present moment lies in the particularly acute form of this contradiction.

The great infrastructural booms of the past – railways, the telegraph, electrification, and the telecom bubble at the turn of the 2000s – also passed through cycles of overaccumulation, credit inflation, and crash. As a rule, however, the physical service life of the elements of fixed capital was comparable to, or significantly exceeded, the time of its economic turnover (payback). AI infrastructure sharply breaks this proportion. Building a gigawatt-scale facility requires a multi-year construction cycle (bureaucratic approvals, laying gas pipelines, grid integration): according to official reports from grid operator PJM Interconnection and studies by Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (LBNL), the volume of data centre connection requests in the largest US power grid has quadrupled, with waiting times stretching out to 2030. Waiting in the interconnection queue alone now takes four to seven years, and factoring in the construction of accompanying power generation stretches the cycle to commissioning to five to eight years. Meanwhile, according to analytical reports from commercial real estate consultancies (CBRE, JLL), the cost of building data centre infrastructure is estimated at $8–10 million per megawatt of capacity, tying capital rigidly to land and concrete. At the same time, the technological core housed inside that facility undergoes rapid moral depreciation in just three to five years. According to semiconductor market trackers and the company’s own official technology roadmaps, an Nvidia H100 graphics accelerator, which cost around $30,000–$40,000 in 2023, loses its commercial attractiveness for training frontier models by the time the Rubin architecture is deployed in 2026, turning, by hyperscaler standards, into an expensive space heater. Public financial filings of technology corporations show that the estimated useful life of server and networking equipment is usually around five to six years, though it varies across companies and asset categories. Yet the accounting depreciation period must not be equated with the period of economic competitiveness of the equipment. With rapid turnover in GPU architectures, older generations of accelerators can lose their advantages in performance and energy efficiency long before physical wear and tear. For analysis, therefore, accounting depreciation and economic devaluation must be tracked separately.

Capital is immobilised for a term comparable to – or even exceeding – the life cycle of the means of production it purchases, and it binds itself not by rational calculation, but under the whip of the coercive laws of competition.

As Karl Marx pointed out, the cyclical movement and periodic renewal of fixed capital form the material basis of the periodicity of industrial cycles:

_«True, periods in which capital is invested differ greatly and far from coincide in time. But a crisis always forms the starting point of large new investments. Therefore, from the point of view of society as a whole, more or less, a new material basis for the next turnover cycle»[^2]._

From this structural gap follow three consequences that form the subject of our article: first, the scale of investment far exceeds the self-financing capacity of individual monopolies; second, the credit system breeds fictitious capital by capitalising revenues from capacities not yet built or utilised; third, competitive struggle drives expansion forward precisely when the profitability of investment becomes unclear and material conditions grow ever tighter.

None of these phenomena can be deduced from the use-value of “automation” as such. All of them are the direct product of a political-economic conflict within capital itself: the conflict between the long horizon of material construction and the headlong pace of moral devaluation.

Sandcastles are built lightly, from someone else’s sand and on someone else’s shore, and the first tide washes them away. Capital builds the same way: on credit, from someone else’s labour, on someone else’s land – and banking on finishing before the tide comes in.

## Does AI Produce Value?

Everything said so far remains formal until the decisive question is posed: where is the surplus value created that must pay back the infrastructure? The starting point here is unchanged: a machine, including a trained model, transfers its own value piecemeal to the product, but creates no new value; new value is created only by living labour – including the labour of engineers, construction workers, and data labellers. According to studies by the International Labour Organization (ILO) and journalistic investigations by outlets such as The Guardian and Time into outsourcing platforms like Scale AI and Sama, by 2025 the global army of labellers filtering and annotating datasets for AI exceeded 10 million people, predominantly in Kenya, India, and the Philippines, where the base rate is often less than $2 an hour.

Marx wrote:

_«_\[…\] _means of production never transfer more value to the product than they themselves lose during the labour process by the destruction of their own use value. If such an instrument has no value to lose, if, in other words, it is not the product of human labour, it transfers no value to the product. It helps to create use value without contributing to the formation of exchange value. In this class are included all means of production supplied by Nature without human assistance, such as land, wind, water, metals in situ and timber in virgin forests»[^3]._

For Marx, labour is productive when a wage labourer creates surplus value for capital – including in the production of services sold as a commodity. Part of the labour employed in circulation – for example, commercial, advertising, and accounting labour – creates no value: it merely accelerates turnover and reduces the costs of circulation, being paid out of already produced surplus value.

_«The general law is that all costs of circulation which arise only from changes in the forms of commodities do not add to their value. They are merely expenses incurred in the realisation of the value or in its conversion from one form into another. The capital spent to meet those costs (including the labour done under its control) belongs among the_ faux frais _of capitalist production. They must be replaced from the surplus product and constitute, as far as the entire capitalist class is concerned, a deduction from the surplus value or surplus product, just as the time a labourer needs for the purchase of his means of subsistence is lost time»[^4]._

The exceptions are transport, storage, and packaging: they continue the process of production within the sphere of circulation and create value.

The productive or unproductive character of labour is determined not by the technical content of the operation performed, nor by the enterprise’s belonging to a particular branch, but by the social form of labour and its relation to capital. One and the same algorithmic system may be applied either to reduce pure costs of circulation or directly in the production of a commodity or commercially sold service. It is therefore impossible to declare in advance that all labour associated with customer service or programming is unproductive: one must examine the concrete process of production and circulation, the form of employment, and the mode of appropriation of the result. Likewise, the saving of labour time by an individual capital does not yet prove an increase in the aggregate mass of surplus value. It may signify only a redistribution of already produced value among competing capitals, unaccompanied by any increase in its total mass.

This distinction is critical for AI. A substantial share of its deployment is directed not into production, but precisely into the costs of circulation: search, recommendations, advertising targeting, transaction processing, document workflows, and customer support. As the official triumphant press releases of the Swedish fintech giant Klarna from early 2024 state, when its AI assistant replaces 700 customer-support staff, handling the workload of 2.3 million hours of customer conversations, it creates not a gram of new value. It merely cuts costs of circulation by $40 million a year, enabling the company to redistribute profit in its own favour. All this trims overhead, accelerates turnover, and raises the rate of profit indirectly – through savings, not through the creation of new value. For an individual capital, the effect is real and tangible. Yet this effect does not by itself increase the aggregate mass of surplus value: one capital’s gain may turn into another’s loss, and savings on wages into the displacement of workers and the expansion of the reserve army of labour.

The practical collision between capitalist expectations and real costs is already beginning to surface in corporate policy. The initial calculation that unlimited access to generative models would automatically deliver a leap in “efficiency” (that is, savings in paid labour time) ran into the colossal cost of those very computing capacities. According to the business press (_Bloomberg_), in autumn 2026 Apple was forced to introduce strict rationing of engineers’ spending on AI usage, cutting the daily limit from $300 to $200, with the prospect of further reductions to $100 for developers and $25 for non-engineering divisions. Similar measures were introduced by Meta after its staff consumed 73.7 trillion tokens in a single month; telling too are leaks about unnamed corporations whose monthly bills for access to Anthropic’s Claude model reached $500 million due to the absence of internal caps.

These figures prove that expenditure on access to cloud computing (appearing to tenant corporations as rising costs) is capable of rapidly swallowing up the very surplus value that capital counted on freeing through the automation of routine tasks. Moreover, the scarcity and rationing of capacity vividly demonstrate the classic Marxist thesis on the subordination of _living labour_ to _dead labour_: programmers and engineers are increasingly compelled to rearrange their personal schedules, adjusting their working hours to windows of AI-token availability. The rhythm of human activity is now dictated not by natural needs and not by the labour contract, but by the algorithmic quotas of a cloud monopolist.

Similar limits are being introduced by Tesla, Uber, and Meta. As industry representatives themselves admit, the mass rollout of AI by no means always yields productivity growth, and in a number of cases the cost of using neural networks (an element of constant capital) proves comparable to the worker’s own wage.

Looking at estimates from systems integrators and specialist media (in particular, data from _Kommersant_), it turns out that corporate subscriptions to frontier models (for example, $150–$250 per month per programmer for Claude Code at Anthropic’s official tariffs), multiplied across thousands of users, weigh heavily on the balance sheet. As AI-centre experts note, the situation is aggravated by the use of AI agents that make repeated, cyclical calls to an API[^5] to complete a single task. For cloud monopolies, this cyclical consumption of tokens serves as an ideal mechanism for continuous revenue extraction, but for tenant corporations it turns into an uncontrollable cost item that rapidly eats away at potential surplus value. As a result, capital is forced to shift to direct rationing of access to the means of production: frontier tools become a scarce resource handed out to staff on a residual basis or as a reward for high output. It turns out that exploiting traditional living labour often remains more profitable for capital than paying the bills of cloud monopolists.

As in any other capitalist sector, technological demand here is secondary. It is put forward by capital not for the sake of computation as such, but primarily as an instrument for saving living labour or capturing market share from a competitor. If the deployment of AI does not secure an increase in productivity and in the mass of surplus value on a social scale sufficient to reproduce the infrastructure created, then the private revenues of individual corporations may be sustained by redistributing already produced value and appropriating rent, yet this still does not mean that the system as a whole pays for itself. In that case, credit claims rest on expectations of future revenues that may fail to find an adequate material basis.

It is along this line that the fundamental political-economic fork runs – determined not by the technological capabilities of neural networks, but by the concrete sphere in which capital is applied. As long as investment is concentrated in the costs of circulation – advertising targeting, trading algorithms, or customer service – it creates no new value. What occurs is merely a redistribution of already produced surplus value among competing monopolies through savings on unproductive outlays.

An altogether different dynamic arises when capital deploys AI directly in the production of the means of subsistence: in agriculture, the food industry, transport logistics, and mass housing construction. As corporate investor presentations show, when computer-vision algorithms are embedded in autonomous John Deere tractors for precision farming or, according to Amazon Robotics reports, direct an army of Sparrow robotic manipulators in warehouses (where they physically sort millions of goods), AI begins to participate in the real production process. By cheapening consumer goods necessary for the physical survival of the proletariat, algorithmic automation objectively lowers the value of reproducing labour power itself (and thus, other things being equal, necessary labour time). As a result, a rise in relative surplus value becomes possible no longer merely for an individual capitalist, but on the scale of capitalist society as a whole. It is precisely at this link that gigantic AI infrastructure first gains a chance to pay for itself materially – yet no longer through the market extraction of part of surplus value from competitors, but through a frontal intensification of the exploitation of the entire working class.

This process does not unfold with mechanical inevitability. Cheapening the consumption basket leads to a real reduction in variable capital (v) only to the extent that capital succeeds in pushing through a corresponding drop in nominal wages. Whether the proletariat can keep part of the gain from increased social productivity in its own hands is wholly a question of the balance of class forces, not of technological determinism. In parallel, algorithmisation triggers a process of deskilling: complex labour is reduced to simple labour, devalued labour power is pushed out into the industrial reserve army, and the time freed up by technology is alienated in full by capital in the form of surplus labour, without expanding genuinely free human time by a single iota. The final outcome is therefore determined by three factors: the sector into which investment is directed, the objective depth of the fall in the value of labour power, and the capacity of the working class to defend its living standards in open confrontation. It is precisely this class dynamic on the material basis of production, rather than speculative swings in stock indices, that will decide the fate of the AI industry.

Thus, the profitability of AI infrastructure may rest on several distinct sources that must not be conflated. The first is surplus value created by productive labour when AI is used in the production of goods and services. The second is the redistribution of already produced surplus value among competing capitals, including through savings on costs of circulation and shifts in market positions. The third is rent – that is, stable excess profit appropriated by raising prices as a result of structural monopoly control over non-reproducible or hard-to-reproduce and/or scarce models, data, software tools, and computing infrastructure. Finally, an individual capital may derive revenue from state subsidies, tax breaks, and shifting part of its infrastructural expenses onto other social actors.

For an individual corporation, all these inflows may participate in forming cash flow and servicing its obligations. For social capital as a whole, however, they carry unequal significance. The redistribution of surplus value and the appropriation of rent allow some capitals to derive income at the expense of others, but do not by themselves increase the aggregate mass of produced surplus value. State support likewise does not automatically create additional value: it alters the distribution of social resources and costs. Therefore, the profitability of an individual data centre operator does not yet prove that the entire system of AI infrastructure generates a sufficient increase in aggregate surplus value for its own reproduction.

## Appropriation Without Creation: Rent

Special attention must be paid to the form in which income is extracted here. A substantial part of the “AI economy” lives not off surplus value produced in production, but off rent:[^6] on model weights, on data, on platform position, on network effects, on cloud access. Marx’s theory of rent – developed for agriculture and extractive industry – applies here in substance: a monopoly over a non-reproducible or hard-to-reproduce and/or scarce resource without which other capitalists cannot manage enables the appropriation of a share of aggregate surplus value regardless of one’s own costs.

This changes the picture. A data centre on its own is a low-margin business: expensive energy, expensive equipment, rapid depreciation, competition for sites. The real rent sits higher up – on the model, on the platform, on access. According to quarterly and annual financial filings (Forms 10-Q and 10-K) submitted to the SEC, Nvidia’s gross margin, which held at an unprecedented 75–78 % in 2024–2025, is the classic empirical expression of this rent, unattainable under conditions of normal competition. That is why capital invests so eagerly in infrastructure: it serves as the material consolidation of a rent-bearing position, a barrier to entry, and a lever in negotiations with creditors and the state. Whoever controls the model also controls the terms on which everyone else gains access to computation. The rent extracted from control over AI models does not exist in isolation from the material basis: it is realised through a gigantic data centre infrastructure and defended by it as an insurmountable barrier to competitors. The bank financing construction, the investment fund accumulating debt, the operator of the computing site, and the corporation owning the model are bound together by far more than market contracts. They are united by a common interest in holding monopoly positions, and that bond is institutionalised through the classic “system of participations”, joint holding companies, and interlocking directorates (personal union). The fact that ING puts a loan tranche in the Stargate project up for auction, Blue Owl operates with these debt instruments, and Oracle manipulates force-majeure notices exposes a single chain in which banking capital has organically fused with the industrial capital of the technology sector. “Financial oligarchy” appears here not as a journalistic metaphor, but as an exact political-economic category – that very form of domination in which monopoly rent on alienated social knowledge merges into one with parasitic income on loan capital.

At the same time, one must take account of the immanent contradiction undermining this rent from within. The spread of open-source AI models can weaken monopoly over algorithmic developments themselves, yet it does not necessarily abolish rent. Control may shift to the owners of computing capacity, cloud infrastructure, datasets, and commercial access channels. As a result, instead of eliminating monopoly, the possibility arises of its relocation to other tiers of the production chain. The question therefore concerns not only the legal openness of models, but social control over the entire system of conditions of their development, training, and deployment.

This exposes an objective historical fork: either the financial oligarchy succeeds in keeping the fruits of the "general intellect" in the vice of private appropriation, or social knowledge breaks through those bounds.[^7] In the second scenario, capital will be left face to face with a gigantic mass of dead labour (the concrete of data centres), bloated fictitious capital of debts, and the pitiless law of moral depreciation demanding immediate return on investment within three years.

The classic formula – ownership of the means of production – is here not merely insufficient, but dangerously insufficient: it points to buildings and machines, that is, precisely to what can be handed over to the state without any change in social relations. The bottleneck of the industry, however, is neither concrete nor buildings. To nationalise data centres without socialising the model, the data, and cloud access means creating a state landlord while preserving private monopoly over the core of the technology – the main means of production in this branch, accumulating alienated social knowledge turned into private rent.

Yet the existence of a source of income does not yet mean that investments pay off. For an individual capital, what is decisive is the ratio between the total cash flow generated by operating the infrastructure and the volume of advanced capital. After deducting current outlays on electricity, maintenance, wages, and other production expenses, the remaining revenue must, over the period of economic operation of the equipment, recoup the investment, cover the servicing of borrowed credit, and yield a profit corresponding to the prevailing rate of return on capital. In other words, it is not enough to collect revenue from renting out computing capacity; accumulated net income must make it possible to recover the invested capital before the equipment loses competitiveness and requires replacement.

At the same time, the payback of an entire data centre cannot be calculated mechanically on a single schedule. Buildings, power installations, cooling systems, servers, and graphics accelerators have different physical service lives and different rates of economic devaluation. The infrastructural shell may remain in use for decades, whereas the technological core will require renewal after only a few years. Project profitability must therefore be assessed separately by major asset groups and then in aggregate – taking into account replacement costs, financing costs, actual capacity utilisation, and the dynamics of computation prices.

For aggregate social capital, a stricter criterion applies. Revenues obtained by individual corporations through rent, interest, profit redistribution, and state support may ensure their private profitability, yet do not automatically increase the aggregate mass of surplus value. On the social level, productive labour must create a sufficient mass of new value and surplus value to reproduce the entire system, including replacing worn-out means of production and maintaining unproductive links. Thus, the private payback of an individual data centre and the reproduction of AI infrastructure as part of social capital are two different questions.

## Overaccumulation, Credit Expansion, and Fictitious Capital

By overaccumulation is meant here the accumulation of capital beyond what can be employed at a sufficient rate of exploitation: an excess of capital and capacity that must be devalued.

AI infrastructure is being built not only out of the accumulated internal funds of technology corporations. Its expansion rests increasingly on credit, bond issues, project finance, and complex relationships among banks, investment funds, data centre operators, and equipment suppliers.

Credit is a necessary element of capitalist reproduction. It makes it possible to mobilise temporarily idle money funds, accelerate the turnover of capital, and carry out investments that an individual enterprise could undertake only after passing through a prolonged stage of prior accumulation.

Yet credit does not eliminate the contradictions of accumulation. On the contrary, it enables production and investment to expand beyond the limits directly set by already accumulated money capital. It thereby accelerates the development of the productive forces while simultaneously heightening the dependence of capitalist reproduction on the future realisation of value.[^8]

At this point it is necessary to turn to Marx’s category of _fictitious capital_.

Fictitious capital represents capitalised claims to future revenues. Shares, bonds, and other financial claims acquire an independent market value, although in themselves they are not additional means of production and create no surplus value. Their valuation depends on expected revenues, interest rates, risk, and the state of the financial market.

More importantly, fictitious capital is a form of existence of loan capital: a title to income circulating independently of the underlying production. One and the same capital does not exist twice – once as actually functioning means of production and a second time as a paper claim on the surplus value they produce.[^9] Illusion does not create a single extra server, yet it creates the semblance of wealth that can be sold.

In the case of AI infrastructure, three interconnected yet non-identical elements must be distinguished.

The first is productive capital: buildings, servers, graphics processors, cooling systems, power installations, and other real means of production.

The second is loan capital: money funds provided by banks and funds to finance construction and equipment purchases.

The third is fictitious capital: titles to future revenues arising in connection with operating the infrastructure and servicing debts.

Let us trace this sequence step by step: it is precisely here that the moment stands out clearly when the material reality of value is supplanted by a paper title of ownership.

1\. _Advancing of loan capital_: a bank or investment fund opens a credit line for construction.

2\. _Conversion of money capital into elements of productive capital_: the developer purchases means of production (equipment, materials) and pays for labour power.

3\. _Formation of the material basis_: a functioning facility emerges – real productive capital.

4\. _Projection of future value_: the operator builds into its calculations the appropriation of future surplus value and rent from leasing out these capacities.

5\. _Capitalisation of expectations_: hypothetical, as yet unproduced revenues turn into fictitious capital, becoming the basis for issuing new debt claims and inflating the corporation’s stock-market capitalisation.[^10]

6\. _Alienation of the financial form_: the market price of these titles of ownership finally detaches itself from the physical utilisation of the facility and begins to obey its own speculative logic, governed primarily by fluctuations in the average rate of interest and risk assessment.

At the fourth step, the fundamental political-economic gap between real production and financial expectation opens up; at the fifth, this gap is institutionalised; at the sixth, fictitious capital acquires an illusory independence from its material foundation, dictating its own laws to it.

The data centre itself, even when not used directly by its owner, continues to function as capital-as-property. By leasing out computing capacity, the owner receives an income, yet this income cannot be automatically equated with a share of surplus value in the strict Marxian sense. Lease payments may include compensation for wear and tear and equipment depreciation, interest on invested capital, profit of enterprise, and a rent component. Their proportions are determined by contract terms, ownership structure, competitive conditions, and the infrastructure owner’s position in the system of production and circulation. To identify the real source of income, one must therefore establish what part of it represents replacement of transferred value, what part stems from the distribution of profit, and what part derives from the appropriation of rent.

Concrete and chips are real; the title to their future revenue is legally real, yet as value it is fictitious. And the capitalisation of expected revenues can exceed many times over the value that the facility is capable of realising. It is precisely this capitalisation of future revenues, detached from the material basis of production, that exposes the critical point at which fictitious capital can no longer mask the fundamental contradiction of accumulation.

The subsequent fate of financial titles depends on how far revenue expectations are borne out by the actual results of operating the infrastructure. If computing capacities are utilised, cash flows ensure the recovery of invested capital and the renewal of equipment, and the profit obtained matches the expected rate of return, financial claims retain a material basis and the capitalisation of future revenues receives real confirmation. If, however, the revenues of individual operators are propped up mainly by intra-industry contracts, rent, subsidies, or continuous debt refinancing, the market value of titles may be sustained for a time without a corresponding growth in aggregate surplus value.

Finally, if actual utilisation and profitability prove insufficient to service obligations and renew equipment, a devaluation of financial claims sets in. It may take the form of a fall in the market value of shares and bonds, debt write-offs, loan restructurings, operator bankruptcies, and asset sales at reduced prices. At the same time, physically existing data centres do not necessarily disappear: they can continue operating after part of the capital is written off, ownership changes hands, and the price of the computation provided falls. That is precisely why the financial collapse of individual owners and the devaluation of fictitious capital are not identical with the physical destruction of productive infrastructure.

Yet behind the abstract credit scheme lies another level of contradiction: a system of mutual obligations within the AI industry itself. One corporation builds the infrastructure, a second finances it, a third supplies the equipment, and expected utilisation is guaranteed by contracts inside that same narrow circle of monopolies. Reported revenue grows rapidly, but then the question arises: what share of this revenue is genuinely realised through external effective demand, and what share represents a mere shifting of advanced capital from one pocket to another?

In itself, this form of fictitious accumulation is nothing new. In 1999–2001, Lucent and Nortel generously extended credit to telecom operators, who used that very money to buy their equipment, sending suppliers’ book revenues skyrocketing. When the credit bubble burst, bad debts boomeranged back onto the manufacturers. The financial pyramids of holding companies on the eve of the 1929 stock-market crash operated on an analogous scheme, as did the railway mania that led to the collapse of Jay Cooke’s banking house in 1873. Marx described this mechanism as a tight chain of mutual payment obligations: the illusion of prosperity is sustained by internal commercial credit, yet the insolvency of even a single link can trigger a chain reaction of crashes.

The scale of this circular guarantee in the 2026-vintage AI industry is reflected in official stock-market filings and the press. Nvidia holds roughly 7 % of the shares of operator CoreWeave, having brought its investment to approximately $2 billion by January 2026 and becoming its second-largest shareholder – while simultaneously committing to purchase $6.3 billion in cloud services from that same company. Moreover, according to official press releases and _Financial Times_ reporting, to finance equipment purchases operators are using the graphics processors themselves as collateral for billion-dollar loans from Wall Street consortia led by Blackstone and Magnetar (so-called GPU-backed financing). That same Nvidia has taken equity stakes in Nscale, Nebius, Safe Superintelligence, and Naver, invested up to $10 billion in Anthropic, and assumed commitments of up to $100 billion toward OpenAI. By July 2026, it carried roughly $46 billion in equity participations on its balance sheet (up from $7 billion a year earlier), alongside an additional $25 billion in investment commitments; the total announced volume of such intra-industry deals over the year exceeded $540 billion. Oracle’s position in the Jupiter project demonstrates the same circularity: there the company acts simultaneously as tenant, joint-venture partner, and developer, while Blue Owl, the fund financing construction, is closely tied to the borrower by a dense web of cross-contracts.[^11]

At this point Marxist analysis demands precision, lest political economy degenerate into journalism. In itself, this circular system of participations proves not the fictitiousness of demand as such, but its extreme structural fragility. Financial obligations inside the circuit are legally real, revenue is recorded on balance sheets, and offsetting claims cancel one another out. Yet precisely because capitals are tightly intertwined, default risk becomes systemically correlated: the fall of a single giant can bring down the entire industry chain. Overproduction and inflated demand are proven differently: by rigorously comparing revenue from external paying customers against the fictitious turnover that the industry generates solely by circulating its own money inside a closed loop.

It is precisely in this configuration that the possibility of a financial bubble arises.

Companies begin building infrastructure on the basis of forecasts of future demand for computing capacity. Creditors advance funds against expected revenues, investors acquire financial claims, and companies’ market valuations rise in anticipation of further market expansion.

Particularly risky is the financing of facilities whose construction begins before agreements are signed with future tenants. In November 2026, the Bank of England drew lenders’ attention to such projects, known in real estate as speculative construction. The regulator inquired into the scale and risks of such financing without automatically declaring it impermissible.

In this case, not only are the future revenues of already operating enterprises capitalised, but also the supposed revenues from facilities that remain to be built and filled with orders.

ING’s sale of a roughly $500 million loan portfolio, including a stake in financing the Stargate facility in New Mexico, is of interest precisely in this context.

By itself, this decision does not prove the existence of bad debts or an already accomplished devaluation of loans. It may be linked to risk management and changes in the structure of the bank’s portfolio. Yet it shows that credit claims on AI infrastructure are already becoming an independent object of redistribution among finance capitals.

Here lies one of the characteristic features of fictitious capital. Its market value can change independently of the immediate state of the productive facilities to whose revenues it lays claim. When expectations worsen, investors seek to rid themselves of financial claims, converting them into money. If problems with debt service arise at the same time, financial devaluation begins to act directly upon the reproduction of real capital.

Thus, credit does not eliminate the gap between the actual production of value and the capitalisation of future revenues. It allows that gap to widen until the contradiction manifests itself in the form of a financial crisis.

In the third volume of _Capital_, Marx demonstrates that credit performs a dual function. It delays the manifestation of the tendency of the rate of profit to fall – and it also turns that tendency into a crisis. It is the mechanism through which the gap reaches its limit. The crisis is resolved by the devaluation of capital. Devaluation is not only a loss, but also the way capitalism restores profitability: by writing off part of its assets and devaluing titles, capital once again renders the rate of profit acceptable. The costs of this restoration are distributed unevenly and fall above all on workers, small holders, and debtors – on those who cannot wait it out. Crisis is functional for capital as a whole and ruinous for many of its bearers. Hence the ferocity of the struggle over who precisely will bear the loss.

AI Infrastructure and the Shift in the Organic Composition of Capital

At the foundation of capitalist production lies the extraction of surplus value from the unpaid labour of wage labourers. In the process of production, capital is divided into constant capital (_c_), embodied in the means of production, and variable capital (_v_), spent on purchasing labour power. The ratio of constant to variable capital characterises the organic composition of capital.

Marx distinguishes the technical composition of capital – the physical mass of means of production per worker – from the value composition, that is, the ratio _c_/_v_. He calls the value composition organic insofar as it is determined by the technical composition and reflects its changes. Undoubtedly, gigawatts, buildings, and graphics processors raise the technical composition. Whether they raise the value composition depends on what happens to the cost of a unit of computation. If it falls faster than the mass of capacity grows, the pressure on the rate of profit is weaker than simple arithmetic of capital intensity suggests. An industry with rapid technical progress and swiftly cheapening output can expand its physical scale without raising – and even while lowering – its value composition.

Marx linked the development of capitalist production with a tendency for the organic composition of capital to rise. Competition compels every capitalist to increase the productivity of labour, introduce machinery, improve technology, and expand the scale of production. To do so, an ever larger share of capital must be invested in means of production, while the relative share of living labour declines.

In the third volume of Capital, this tendency is examined in connection with the law of the tendency of the rate of profit to fall. The rate of profit is determined by the ratio of surplus value to the total advanced capital.

Other things being equal, a rise in the organic composition of capital puts downward pressure on the rate of profit. Yet Marx did not treat this process as a mechanical relation: the operation of the law is accompanied by counteracting factors, among them a rise in the degree of exploitation of labour, the depression of wages below the value of labour power, the cheapening of the elements of constant capital, relative surplus population, foreign trade, and the growth of joint-stock capital; Marx examines the effect of turnover on the annual rate of profit separately.

In AI infrastructure, another factor acts with particular force – moral depreciation – and it too turns into a vulnerability. Server equipment can lose economic competitiveness after just three to five years and, with the succession of chip generations, lose value long before physical wear and tear. This process must be distinguished from accounting depreciation, whose periods are set by accounting rules and may differ. This rapid moral depreciation forces capital to demand an immediate return on investment long before the machines physically break down. Accelerated renewal of fixed capital can raise the annual rate of profit, yet it also means that investments do not have time to pay off: capital is sunk into means of production that are devalued before they yield a return. Acceleration of turnover and devaluation are here two sides of a single process.

These tendencies act in opposite directions, and their net balance is not mechanically predetermined. The enormous cost of concrete shells, power infrastructure, and cooling systems sharply increases the mass of constant capital (_c_), pushing the organic composition upward. At the same time, the rapid cheapening of computation itself per unit of capacity acts as a classic counteracting factor: it devalues the elements of constant capital, pulling the value composition down. The displacement of living labour from the direct servicing of data centres compresses variable capital (_v_), which, other things being equal, raises the _c_/_v_ ratio. Conversely, rising labour costs in other, more labour-intensive links of the global production chain increase the mass of variable capital, braking the rise of that ratio. Finally, moral depreciation must not be equated with an acceleration of capital turnover. It signifies the premature loss of value of still physically serviceable means of production and may entail writing off the untransferred portion of their value. Accelerated replacement of equipment, in turn, can increase the frequency of capital advances and the costs of reproducing fixed capital. Therefore, the effects of turnover time, depreciation, and moral devaluation on the annual rate of profit must be accounted for separately. In AI infrastructure, these processes are closely intertwined, yet do not coincide. That is precisely why simple arithmetic of capital intensity proves nothing. By itself, the unprecedented capital intensity of AI infrastructure does not yet mean an automatic fall in the rate of profit; it merely pushes the political-economic tension between these counteracting forces to the limit. One must examine the relationship among investment, actual capacity utilisation, revenue, equipment maintenance costs, the cost of labour power, and productivity dynamics.

Nevertheless, the scale of investment reveals a characteristic tendency.

Creating infrastructure for artificial intelligence requires building vast data centres, purchasing graphics processors, servers, and cooling systems, laying utilities, constructing power plants, and expanding grids. The Stargate project, announced in January 2025, envisaged creating up to 10 GW of infrastructure in the United States by 2029. In April 2026, OpenAI announced that it had secured access to more than 10 GW of computing infrastructure, exceeding Stargate’s initial target. Yet announced, contracted, under-construction, and actually commissioned capacities represent different metrics. The company reported an expansion of secured infrastructure, but these figures cannot be interpreted as confirmation that 10 GW of operational, fully loaded data centres are in place.

Servicing such facilities requires far fewer workers than traditional industries of comparable capital investment.

Yet the peculiarity of AI infrastructure is not that it ceases to depend on living labour – for even the most mechanised, automated, and robotised enterprise still depends on the use of living labour, both directly, insofar as it is compelled to reproduce the process of exploitation, the process of extracting surplus value, by hiring a certain number of highly skilled workers, and indirectly, because the value of all the equipment it uses necessarily contains the value of the living labour embodied in producing that commodity in previous cycles.

This peculiarity of the AI sector – as indeed of other branches with a high organic composition of capital – lies in the fact that vast investments in means of production must secure excess profit for high-technology enterprises by “flowing” to them from enterprises with outdated equipment and, accordingly, a higher individual value of the commodity.

Surplus value is created not only at the enterprise where a particular capital operates. The profit of an individual capital is formed within the distribution of aggregate surplus value produced by social labour (through the equalisation of profits into an average rate).

At this point a contradiction arises. Capital is compelled continuously to raise the productivity of labour and the technical equipment of production, yet its self-expansion still depends on the exploitation of living labour. Automation can shorten necessary labour time, increase relative surplus value, and simultaneously raise the organic composition of capital. These tendencies act together and intensify the contradictions of capitalist accumulation.

## Conclusion. The Payback of AI Infrastructure

The current market “correction” gives no grounds for declaring the final collapse of the AI bubble. ING’s loan portfolio sale, falling tech share prices, political demands to restrict data centre construction, and power-supply problems at individual projects testify to strain in a specific segment of capitalist accumulation, yet do not prove a general crisis of reproduction. Such a conclusion requires establishing how the actual profitability of infrastructure relates to the volume of advanced capital, the economic service life of equipment, debt burdens, and the necessity of continuous technological renewal.

The analysis carried out allows us to formulate a criterion of payback. An individual data centre or AI infrastructure operator can be profitable if revenues from operating capacity, after covering current expenses, allow it to recoup invested capital, service debts, finance equipment replacement, and secure a profit matching the prevailing rate of return. For this, growth in revenue or computing capacity utilisation alone is not enough: cash flow must be sufficient across the entire economic cycle of the relevant assets. This contradiction manifests itself with particular sharpness in graphics accelerators and server equipment, whose moral devaluation may occur long before physical wear and tear. If equipment fails to pay for itself before losing competitiveness, subsequent renewal turns not into an expansion of accumulation, but into an additional burden requiring a fresh advance of capital.

However, the profitability of individual enterprises is not equivalent to the payback of AI infrastructure on the scale of social capital. Individual corporations can earn profits through the redistribution of already produced surplus value, the appropriation of rent, interest, state subsidies, and shifting part of infrastructural costs onto other social actors. Intra-industry contracts and mutual investments can likewise sustain revenues and market capitalisation without securing a corresponding increase in external effective demand. Yet these mechanisms create no additional aggregate surplus value. On the social level, the decisive question remains whether productive labour provides a sufficient mass of new value and surplus value to reproduce the entire system, including replacing constant capital, renewing equipment, and maintaining unproductive links.

It is precisely here that the boundary runs between real accumulation and the expansion of fictitious capital. If expected revenues are confirmed by real cash flows, financial titles retain a material basis. If, however, capitalisation outpaces the infrastructure’s capacity to produce and appropriate income sufficient to service obligations and renew means of production, a gap opens between the value of financial claims and the actual possibilities of realising them. This gap may be sustained for a time by credit expansion, intra-industry deals, rent, and state support, yet such mechanisms can delay devaluation, not eliminate its causes. When expected profitability is not confirmed, the crisis takes the form of debt write-offs, falling values of financial titles, bankruptcies of individual operators, and devaluation of productive capital. At the same time, physical infrastructure may survive and pass to other owners at a reduced valuation.

Therefore, the question of whether AI infrastructure will pay for itself cannot be answered either by an unconditional denial or by pointing to the scale of technological progress and announced investments. Its payback is possible, but depends on the concrete balance among labour productivity, sources of appropriated income, financing costs, capacity utilisation, rates of moral devaluation, and capital’s ability to recoup investments before the next technological renewal. The tendencies examined clearly expose the structural contradictions of the AI boom, yet do not provide sufficient grounds for a final verdict on the aggregate profitability of the infrastructure built. It is precisely this contradiction between the scale of advanced capital and the uncertainty of its actual recovery – rather than an isolated drop in stock quotes – that constitutes the political-economic content of the current AI boom.
