**_The Class Struggle in the world_**

# Ebb Tide After the High Tide: The Labour Movement Between the Two World Wars

## Part I

## From Offensive to Retreat: The Exhaustion of the Revolutionary Wave

The years 1917–1921 were a period in which the world system of imperialism faced the direct threat of proletarian revolution in several centres simultaneously. However, the victory of October remained isolated. In Germany – the country with the most developed and organised proletariat on the continent – the November Revolution of 1918 overthrew the monarchy and gave rise to an extensive network of Workers’ and Soldiers’ Councils, but did not develop into a dictatorship of the proletariat. The leadership of Social Democracy played a decisive role in this: the Ebert–Scheidemann government, relying on the Freikorps (volunteer paramilitary units), crushed the January uprising of 1919 in Berlin, killing its leaders – Rosa Luxemburg and Karl Liebknecht – and subsequently suppressed the Soviet republics in Bremen, Bavaria and Brunswick. The Communist Party of Germany (KPD), which had only emerged at the turn of 1918–1919, proved politically unprepared for these struggles: a Bolshevik-type party capable of leading an uprising had simply not had time to take shape by the time the objective situation demanded immediate action.

The explanation for this seemingly paradoxical contrast – why backward, less proletarian Russia achieved what incomparably more developed Germany had failed to achieve – was given by Lenin himself in December 1919, long before various theorists of the defeats of revolutions in Western Europe began to reflect on the question: _«Our banking on the world revolution, if you can call it that, has on the whole been fully justified. But from the point of view of the speed of its development we have endured an exceptionally difficult period; we have seen for ourselves that the revolution’s development in more advanced countries has proved to be considerably slower, considerably more difficult, considerably more complicated. This should not surprise us for it was naturally easier for a country such as Russia to start a socialist revolution than it is for the advanced countries. But, in any case, this slower, more complicated, more zigzag development of the socialist revolution in Western Europe has burdened us with incredible difficulties»_.[^1]

Lenin linked this asymmetry precisely to the difference in the strength of capitalism: the weaker, more backward and less organically integrated into society capitalism is, the easier it is to strike the first decisive blow against it, but the harder it is subsequently to hold on to power and build socialism on its ruins; conversely, the more mature and deeply rooted in the very fabric of society capitalism is (as in the countries of Western Europe), the harder it is to overthrow it with a single blow, but the firmer and more productive will be the foundation on which the victorious proletariat can build.

A decade later, reflecting on the concrete experience of defeats in Germany and Italy, Antonio Gramsci, in his Prison Notebooks, written in a fascist prison, gave this Leninist idea a sociological formulation. In Russia, he wrote, the state was everything, whilst civil society was primordial and gelatinous: behind the autocracy, and subsequently behind the façade of the Provisional Government, there was practically nothing but an apparatus of pure coercion. It was no coincidence that Lenin described Kerensky, who by the summer of 1917 had become the embodiment of this façade, as a figure who played the role of a balalaika in deceiving the workers and peasants – an empty, hollow instrument devoid of any real weight. It was precisely for this reason that a “war of manoeuvre” – a direct assault on this apparatus – proved sufficient to seize power. But for the same reason, holding on to power proved so difficult: behind the shattered state there was no mature, organically developed capitalist foundation on which to build further. In Western Europe, Gramsci continued, following a line of reasoning of his own but faithful to Leninist logic, the state was backed by a powerful system of “trenches and fortifications” within civil society – trade unions, political parties, the press, the Church, the entire mechanism of the everyday production of consent – and therefore a single assault on the state apparatus proved insufficient: a prolonged “war of position” was required, for which neither the KPD in 1918–1923 nor any of the subsequent vanguards examined in this article proved structurally prepared.

This historical fact reveals a pattern that is the exact mirror image of the Russian experience. Where the objective preconditions for revolution (the concentration of the proletariat and the level of development of the productive forces) were incomparably more advanced than in war-ravaged Russia, it was precisely the absence of a revolutionary leadership forged in good time that proved fatal. Here we encounter the reverse of the formula used in the previous article in this series to describe the defeats of the 1970s and 1980s: the subjective factor, embodied in the party, proved decisive not through its suppression from outside, but through a catastrophic delay in its formation.

However, it would be a gross theoretical error to attribute this fateful delay solely to the subjective sluggishness or personal miscalculations of German Marxists. The subjective factor risks becoming an empty idealist abstraction unless it is traced back to its most material roots.

By 1914, Social Democracy and its trade unions constituted not merely the bearers of a mistaken ideology, but a colossal material force – a gigantic bureaucratic apparatus with its own funds, property, press and thousands of officials, firmly entrenched in the structure of the imperialist state. It was physically impossible to wrest millions of workers from the influence of this apparatus overnight. Consequently, this very “subjective” immaturity of the proletarian vanguard was a direct _reflection_ of the objective stability of Western capitalism. The fact that the revolutionary party failed to take shape in time was directly determined by the degree of maturity of the mode of production: the level of capital concentration, the pace of proletarianisation and the bourgeoisie’s ability to bribe broad sections of the _labour_ aristocracy at the expense of imperialist rent. It was precisely these material conditions, rather than the abstract will of political leaders, that dictated the limits of the vanguard’s capabilities in interwar Europe.

The isolation and material exhaustion of the October Revolution itself, and the subsequent degeneration of the Bolshevik Party and the _semi-state_, were first and foremost the result of an objective defeat, of being _broken by deprivation and hunger_, rather than of a strategic choice: here, as demonstrated by the separate series of articles in the previous and current issues devoted to the lessons of the defeat of October, the point was not that the Bolsheviks _could have acted differently_ while the same objective conditions remained in place. But it was precisely this degenerated state, already bourgeois in its social nature and no longer a dictatorship of the proletariat, that, from the mid-1920s onwards, imposed strategies on foreign communist parties through the Comintern – strategies dictated not by the needs of world revolution, but by the foreign policy interests of the USSR and the internal factional struggles within the All-Union Communist Party (Bolsheviks) itself. Hence the Anglo-Russian Committee, the alliance with the Kuomintang, and the “Third Period”, which emerged as a by-product of Bukharin’s defeat.[^2] In contrast to the Russian catastrophe, the defeats in Italy, Britain, China and Germany were not a matter of objective impossibility, but of a _subjectively imposed political line_.

As in the case of the period that began after the end of the Second World War, which we examined in the previous issue of our journal, the facts presented in this article point not to a decline, but to varying degrees of intensity and different trajectories of the outcome of the class struggle, depending on one and the same factor – whether or not the proletariat possesses autonomous revolutionary leadership, free from opportunist vacillations. The historical experience of the interwar decades confirms with particular force the thesis we formulated with regard to a later period: neither the spontaneous militancy of the masses nor the objective crisis of capitalism (the Great Depression, the collapse of the liberal model) can, in themselves, open the way to revolution. On the contrary, in the absence of a world communist party, defeats lead either to outright fascism (Italy, Germany), or to the exhaustion of revolutionary potential within the “democratic” institutional framework of the capitalist state itself (Britain, the United States), or to the physical extermination of the most class-conscious elements of the proletariat at the hands of its formal “allies” and false comrades (China, Spain).

## The Class Struggle in the USSR: From Kronstadt to the Stalinist Counter-Revolution

The defeat of the world revolution had as its direct consequence the degeneration of the dictatorship of the proletariat in isolated Russia. The Kronstadt uprising of March 1921 marked the _de facto_ and symbolic turning point in this process.

The economic and demographic collapse that had engulfed Soviet Russia by 1921 was unprecedented: gross industrial output had fallen to 13.9 per cent of its 1913 level, Petrograd had lost two-thirds of its pre-war population, and the city's mortality rate had risen almost fourfold. It was against this backdrop of total archaicisation and the dissolution of the proletariat into the peasant mass that the suppression of the Kronstadt uprising took place.

From the standpoint of various currents of left communism, such as the KAPD and the _“Rabochaya Pravda”_ group, and, after 1923, Myasnikov's group, the demands of the Kronstadt sailors and the striking workers of Petrograd – “Soviets without Bolsheviks”, freedom of speech for left-wing socialist parties and the release of political prisoners – represented a desperate attempt by the proletariat to defend Soviet democracy against the dictatorship of the party-state apparatus.

The Bolshevik leadership, for its part, faced with the country's total devastation and hostile encirclement, regarded any armed uprising – irrespective of the subjectively left-wing slogans of the insurgents – as an objective threat that opened the way to a White Guard counter-revolution. The Bolsheviks also pointed to the changing composition of the Kronstadt garrison: the politically committed proletarians of 1917 had been replaced by conscripts of peasant origin, whose discontent stemmed primarily from the burden of grain requisitioning in the countryside. Particular attention should also be paid to the fact that unity within Kronstadt itself was far from complete: around half of the local Bolshevik organisation left the party, while some 300 communists and Komsomol members refused to recognise the authority of the Provisional Revolutionary Committee (PRC) and conducted agitation and sabotage against it, finding support among sections of the non-party workers, sailors and Red Army soldiers. Nor should we overlook the appeal issued by the PRC itself on 13 March, calling for _«no means of obtaining external assistance»_ to be ruled out. These facts raise questions not only about the class composition of the insurgents, but also about the objective political trajectory of their leadership – the very trajectory from which Lenin, in his polemic with Martov, derived the argument that the slogan “Soviets without Bolsheviks” was tactically advantageous to the White _émigrés_, irrespective of the subjective intentions of the Kronstadt insurgents themselves.

The Italian left communists (the journal Bilan and Mitchell’s series of articles, “Problems of the Transition Period” (1936–1937)) drew a conclusion from the Kronstadt tragedy whose starting point was, in essence, a thesis advanced by Lenin himself a couple of months before the uprising, during the trade union debate: the state that emerges after a proletarian revolution is a _«workers’ state with a bureaucratic twist to it»[^3]_, not a form of power identical with the dictatorship of the proletariat; and, Lenin argued, workers have the right to defend themselves against such a state in the literal sense of the word. However, Lenin himself refused to extend this logic to Kronstadt: whereas the trade union debate concerned the position of the proletariat in production, within the structures of that same state, in March 1921, in his assessment, what was at stake was not the defence of class organisations, but _the question of proletarian_ _power_ and the military security of the revolution, against the backdrop of the Tambov uprising, famine and the real threat of foreign intervention.[^4]

Be that as it may, the suppression of the Kronstadt uprising coincided with the ban on factions at the Tenth Congress of the RCP(b) and the introduction of the NEP, as well as, as we noted earlier, _«the collapse of the revolutionary wave in Europe»_, which made _«1921 a turning point on the road to the defeat of both the Russian and world revolutions»_.[^5] This political turning point was inextricably linked to the material collapse described above. The physical exhaustion and deurbanisation of the proletarian vanguard created a vacuum that was inevitably filled by a new force – the bourgeois state-party apparatus. Having absorbed declassed elements, careerists and people of peasant origin, this apparatus became detached from the proletarian masses and began to act in interests radically different from those of the working class.

Nevertheless, the class struggle in the USSR did not cease during the 1920s, but took the form of predominantly covert resistance, with only occasional open manifestations (the mass strikes of 1923, triggered by a crisis of sales and delays in wage payments).

In the 1930s, with the onset of forced industrialisation, the trade unions were definitively transformed from organisations defending workers’ interests into an appendage of the economic apparatus. One of their principal tasks became the campaign to increase labour productivity (as exemplified by the Stakhanovite movement). The working class of the USSR was subjected to draconian labour discipline: the introduction of internal passports (1932) and the imposition of criminal liability for lateness and absenteeism (the laws of 1938–1940).[^6] Isolated instances of worker resistance – such as the Teykovo and Vichuga strikes of 1932 against reductions in ration entitlements – were suppressed by the OGPU[^7] using armed force.

The latter two strikes broke out almost simultaneously in the textile-producing districts of Ivanovo Region and constitute the best-documented episode of open workers’ resistance during the First Five-Year Plan, thanks to the OGPU’s archival reports. The immediate cause was a sharp reduction, beginning in early April 1932, in bread and food ration entitlements under the rationing system, against the backdrop of a general supply crisis triggered by forced collectivisation: workers could obtain bread only at fixed prices, whilst all other foodstuffs had to be purchased on the market at prices that had risen many times over. As a result, in the words of the workers themselves, recorded in OGPU reports, their children were wasting away from malnutrition, without even seeing milk or sugar.

In Vichuga, a strike involving around 10,000 workers broke out at several textile enterprises between 5 and 13 April 1932 and quickly escalated into an open uprising: workers stormed and ransacked the building of the city committee of the All-Union Communist Party (Bolsheviks), the local OGPU office and the post office; clashed with mounted militia attempting to disperse the crowd; and beat the head of the city militia and his deputy, breaking into the “militia” headquarters and freeing detainees from their cells. The strikers’ actions were so well organised and coordinated that the OGPU authorities, in their reports, conjectured that an underground organisation directing the uprising existed in the city – a supposition, however, for which no documentary evidence has been found.

At the same time, separate centres of protest emerged in Teykovo, Lezhnevo and Puchezh. According to a report on strike activity in the Ivanovo-Voznesensk Industrial District, the strike in Teykovo lasted from 8 to 15 April 1932, that in Lezhnevo from 10 to 13 April, and that in Puchezh from 14 to 15 April. The number of strikers was estimated at approximately 2,000 in Teykovo, 2,000 in Lezhnevo and 1,000 in Puchezh, respectively.

In Teykovo, the protests culminated in what became known as the “hunger marches” – processions of workers heading towards the regional centre of Ivanovo-Voznesensk to demand the restoration of the previous food ration levels. Groups of protesters set out from Teykovo, Vichuga and Lezhnevo; the largest group, numbering several hundred people, set out from Teykovo. The authorities attempted to prevent the movement from spreading further: according to available information, an order was issued instructing railway staff not to stop trains bound for Ivanovo-Voznesensk at the stations along the route, in order to prevent additional participants from joining the march. The march itself was halted by the authorities on the outskirts of the city: the workers, exhausted by the long journey on foot, were forcibly put aboard a train that had been prepared for the purpose and sent back.

During the same days, peasants from the surrounding areas also began to join the protests, organising so-called _volynki_ on collective farms – work slowdowns and other forms of collective resistance without formally suspending work. Taken together, the protests in Vichuga, Teykovo, Lezhnevo and Puchezh testify to the widespread discontent caused by the food crisis and to the fact that social tensions affected both industrial workers and the rural population.

The unrest was suppressed by OGPU forces and troops deployed to the region by mid-April; the strike in Vichuga came to an end partly of its own accord, as the workers became physically and psychologically exhausted. A number of popular journalistic accounts[^8] claim that several hundred workers were killed during the suppression of the unrest in Vichuga. However, this figure is corroborated neither by the published OGPU archival reports nor by specialist academic historiography on the subject, and should therefore be treated with caution as an unverified claim rather than an established fact. The reaction of the USSR’s top leadership was conspicuously evasive: an article entitled “Immediate Reorganisation Is Essential”, published in Pravda on 31 May 1932 and written by Emelyan Yaroslavsky – one of the leading figures in the VKP(b)’s press apparatus and a prominent associate of J. V. Stalin – sharply criticised the previous party leadership of Ivanovo Region, without mentioning the strikes or their underlying food-related causes even once. Nevertheless, the authorities were forced to respond: shortly after the April events, the opening of collective farm markets and the allocation of individual plots of land to workers for vegetable gardens were authorised throughout the country. Since the capitalist character of the USSR’s economy remained unchanged, this measure should in no way be regarded as a “restoration of the market”. On the contrary, it was a clear tactical manoeuvre by the state-party bourgeoisie: in order to reduce the costs of reproducing labour power and defuse class anger, the bourgeoisie shifted a substantial part of the burden of supplying the proletariat with essential foodstuffs onto the proletariat itself (through household vegetable gardens) and onto small commodity producers. This made it possible to preserve intact the principal mechanisms of exploitation and the appropriation of surplus product required for forced industrialisation, whilst avoiding a full-scale social explosion.

Vichuga and Teykovo were tellingly not isolated incidents, but merely the most acute manifestations of a much broader wave of conflicts between labour and capital: according to an OGPU report dated 9 August 1932, no fewer than 454 strikes caused by dissatisfaction with wages were recorded across the country in the first six months of that year alone. Of these, 125 were prompted by wage arrears, 111 by reductions in pay rates, 166 by low wages and 52 by the failure to provide workers with special work clothing. The April events in Ivanovo Region were therefore not an anomaly, but rather the tip of the iceberg of a much more systematic, albeit almost invariably localised and spontaneous, resistance by the working class of the USSR to forced industrialisation at the expense of its own standard of living.

Whereas in the USSR the defeat of the revolution resulted in its slow degeneration from within, accompanied by agony and terror, in Western Europe the fate of the revolutionary wave of 1917–1921 was decided much more swiftly and openly. Let us turn to one of its most striking episodes – the factory occupations in Italy, where workers’ control over production arguably reached its greatest depth and organisational maturity in the whole of Western Europe, only to be crushed almost immediately.

## The «Two Red Years»: Factory Occupations and the Birth of Pre-emptive Counter-Revolution

The movement unfolded against the backdrop of an economic collapse mirroring that in Germany: the demobilisation of around 4 million soldiers flooded the labour market with an army of unemployed workers, which had exceeded 2 million by 1919, whilst war loans and monetary expansion had devalued the lira by the end of 1920 to one-sixth of its pre-war value.

Membership of the General Confederation of Labour (_Confederazione Generale del Lavoro_, CGL), the country's main trade union confederation, grew from 250,000 in 1918 to 2.2 million in 1920, whilst the Federation of Metalworkers (_Federazione Impiegati Operai Metallurgici_, FIOM) had grown to 160,000 members. In September 1920, around 400,000–500,000 workers, primarily in Turin, where factory councils were being established under the leadership of Gramsci and the newspaper L’Ordine Nuovo, occupied factories and established workers’ control over production. The bourgeoisie subsequently described these weeks as the “great fear” – a moment when the old liberal ruling circles demonstrated their inability to defend the class domination of capital by conventional means.

At the same time, this episode lays bare the same structural contradiction as the Lip affair half a century later[^9], but on a vastly greater scale: factory occupations without a unified revolutionary party capable of linking them to a nationwide struggle for power were doomed to institutional integration and defeat. The leadership of the Italian Socialist Party (PSI) and the CGL refused to support the transformation of the economic occupation of factories into a political uprising. Prime Minister Giovanni Giolitti proposed a compromise (partial satisfaction of economic demands and formal trade union control over production); the workers’ councils were dismantled, and the movement was brought to an end by late September 1920.

The leaders of the ISP and the CGL justified their refusal to escalate the struggle by citing objective factors: Turin’s isolation, the passivity of the peasantry in the South and the lack of weapons. However, the left wing of the Socialist Party drew a fundamentally different lesson from this defeat. At the same time, the defeat of the 1920 movement brought the question of the relationship between factory councils and the political organisation of the proletariat into sharper focus. Gramsci, who placed central emphasis on the councils as organs of workers’ self-government and the transformation of production, and Bordiga, who insisted on the primacy of a centralised Communist Party in the struggle for political power, proceeded from different conceptions of the path to revolution. It would, however, be an oversimplification to assume that the defeat automatically led Gramsci to acknowledge Bordiga’s correctness, or that their positions merged into a single conception. The founding of the Communist Party of Italy (Partito Comunista d’Italia, PCd’I) in Livorno in January 1921 was the outcome of a broader process of political differentiation within the ISP, in which Bordiga’s supporters played the leading organisational role, whilst Gramsci’s supporters participated in the formation of the PCd’I while retaining their own understanding of the respective roles of the councils and the party.

The political consequence of the workers’ defeat was not merely a restoration of the _status quo_, but a qualitatively new development in bourgeois tactics: having recognised that legal and parliamentary methods no longer guaranteed the effective suppression of the proletariat, the dominant sections of the Italian bourgeoisie – first the agrarian bourgeoisie in the Po Valley, and subsequently the industrial bourgeoisie – turned to organised extra-parliamentary violence as an instrument of pre-emptive counter-revolution, financing and arming the Blackshirts. As early as 1922, activists of Turin’s “Red Two Years” were murdered, including the anarcho-syndicalist Pietro Ferrero and the communist Carlo Berruti. In October of that year, fascism established itself in power on a national scale.

However, from the standpoint of the class nature of the state in the imperialist era – characterised by _reaction across the board_ – this transition did not create a _qualitatively_ new regime: fascism and liberal democracy remained two forms of one and the same dictatorship of the bourgeoisie. It was precisely for this reason that the only correct proletarian position was to reject any alliance with the “democratic” bourgeoisie under the slogan of defending the old order against the new. The transition to fascism itself became possible only because the revolutionary situation was squandered from within the labour movement: the reformist leadership of the ISP and the CGL, despite having all the conditions necessary to turn the factory occupations into a seizure of power, chose to capitulate, thereby giving the bourgeoisie the time and space to regroup. The defeat of the “Red Two Years” was thus inflicted upon the proletariat first and foremost by its own indecisive leadership – and only secondarily through the manoeuvres of the capitalist class.

At the same time, the bourgeoisie did not act as a monolithic entity, overcome by fear and instinctively turning to terror. Fascism became a political form serving the specific material interests of the united factions of capital: the heavy industry of the North, which urgently needed state contracts and rearmament; the agrarian Junkers, who demanded the suppression of agricultural workers’ trade unions; and financial capital, whose principal aim was the radical “disciplining” of labour and the destruction of workers’ organisations in order to restore the rate of profit following the post-war crisis of profitability. Those factions of the bourgeoisie that did not support this course would become the driving force behind “anti-fascism”.

Here, too, we must establish a theoretically important point: fascism is a specific response by a _section_ of the bourgeoisie to a situation in which the proletariat demonstrated its _revolutionary potential_ but failed to realise it owing to the absence of party leadership. The class energy that was crushed but not extinguished at the height of the upsurge is turned against the proletariat itself in the form of organised terror carried out by petty-bourgeois and declassed masses placed at the service of big capital.

## The Destruction of the Working Class’s Fighting Core: The 1926 General Strike in Britain

Whilst the crushing of the Italian proletariat took the form of open fascism, in Britain the ruling class chose a course that structurally anticipated the crushing of the miners under Thatcher sixty years later[^10] – a targeted, premeditated assault on the most organised section of the working class.

The background to these events was the so-called “Black Friday” of 15 April 1921. After the mines, which had been nationalised for the duration of the war, were returned to private ownership, their owners immediately cut wages and lengthened the working day. However, the railway and transport trade unions, which had been linked to the miners through the “Triple Alliance” since 1914, refused to support the strike at the decisive moment. The miners found themselves isolated and were literally starved back down the pits.

The crisis in the British coal industry was exacerbated by Britain’s return to the gold standard in 1925, which made British coal and steel more expensive on the international market. Against this backdrop of general impoverishment, when soldiers returning from the front to a “land fit for heroes” found themselves facing unemployment, the mine owners decided in 1925–1926 to repeat their earlier manoeuvre: they demanded a 13.5 per cent cut in wages and an increase in the working day from seven to eight hours.

This time, however, the bourgeoisie had made thorough preparations well in advance: whilst the trade union leadership dithered, the government placed the police, army and navy on standby, while the right-wing Organisation for the Maintenance of Supplies (OMS), staffed mainly by representatives of the “middle class” (retired army officers, financial clerks and university students), was incorporated into the state apparatus to organise strike-breaking. In response to the lockout, the General Council of the Trades Union Congress (TUC) called a general strike in support of the miners on 1 May 1926 – the largest in the history of the British labour movement. It brought out between 1.5 and 2 million workers in transport, printing, metalworking, construction and the energy sector, in staggered waves controlled from above. This was in addition to approximately 1.2 million miners, who were already locked out; the miners’ lockout continued until November 1926.

The strike began on 4 May and lasted nine days, hence its name, the “Nine-Day Strike”. The TUC General Council formally retained control of the strike until its capitulation on 12 May. The strike laid bare the profound contradiction between the militancy of the rank and file and the institutional role of the TUC leadership.

The latter’s capitulation in 1926 cannot be explained by the “cowardice” of its leaders. A historical-materialist analysis points to the material roots of British reformism – the phenomenon of the labour aristocracy. The trade union bureaucracy has historically rested on the super-profits that the British bourgeoisie derived from colonial exploitation and its monopoly position on the world market. This privileged stratum of the working class had much to lose from the collapse of the capitalist empire, and this determined the class betrayal of the leadership at the critical moment.

In practice, the formally centralised strike leadership (the Strike Organisation Committee, headed by Ernest Bevin) relied at local level on Councils of Action, which had emerged spontaneously or on the basis of existing Trades Councils. These organised mass picketing, published their own strike bulletins, provided supplies to strikers and their families, took control of the movement of people and goods in certain areas, and established their own Workers’ Defence Corps and special picketing units to confront the police. However, with few exceptions, these councils never managed to unite into a nationwide structure or free themselves from the restraining influence of the TUC. On 11 May, a High Court ruling in the case brought by the miners’ union declared the strike illegal. On 12 May, having secured no guarantees from the government or employers and fearing that the economic strike might develop into a political crisis of power, the TUC General Council – without consulting the strikers themselves – abruptly capitulated and called off the general strike.

The news was met by the workers with disbelief and anger. Significantly, on 13 May – the day after the strike was called off – a considerable number of workers remained on strike, clearly demonstrating that the class had retained its fighting spirit. Nevertheless, the trade union apparatus and the state, acting in concert, forced the workers back into the factories, leaving the miners to continue the struggle alone until November 1926. By then, forced to sell off their possessions in order to survive and receiving only meagre assistance (including from the USSR), the miners had been effectively broken by starvation and accepted the mine owners’ demands for district-level rather than national agreements, and for wage cuts.

The leaders of the TUC General Council justified their capitulation by citing the threat of the impending sequestration of trade union funds and their complete lack of any strategy for an open confrontation with the state. The principal counterpoint to this course was the position taken by A. J. Cook, the radical miners’ leader. He embodied the militancy of the strike and, to the very end, called for the struggle to continue under the slogan_: «Not a penny off the wages, not a minute on the working day!»._

Here, a methodological qualification is necessary concerning the role of political parties. The left wing of the bourgeoisie, represented by the Labour Party (whose leader, Ramsay MacDonald, had himself invoked the government’s Emergency Powers Act against striking dockers as early as 1924), called for moderation and offered no support to the strike. The leadership of the Communist Party of Great Britain (CPGB), meanwhile, had been partially arrested on the eve of the strike on charges of incitement to mutiny, while the Comintern and the All-Union Communist Party (Bolsheviks) had placed their hopes in the “left-wing” leadership of the TUC through the so-called Anglo-Russian Committee of 1925 – a policy that proved politically untenable. Nevertheless, the CPGB itself subsequently attributed the defeat solely to the “betrayal by the right-wing leadership” of the TUC – as though it were an accidental occurrence that could be remedied simply by replacing one set of officials with another. This interpretation evades the central question: the TUC did not so much “betray” the workers at a moment of weakness or out of the cowardice of individual leaders as fulfil the structural function of a mediating body between labour and capital within the bourgeois state itself – the very function for which trade unions had historically developed. The issue was not the personal qualities of the TUC leaders, but the very nature of the trade union as an organ of the capitalist system, whose function is to negotiate the terms on which labour power is bought and sold and, consequently, to maintain social peace and “discipline” the proletariat. The working class must not seek to “conquer” the trade union apparatus from within or demand revolutionary action of an institution incapable of carrying it out.

The position of the CPGB itself proved deeply contradictory. Whilst radicalising workers through the National Minority Movement, founded in 1924 with the CPGB’s participation and under the influence of the Comintern, it simultaneously confined them within the framework of the official trade unions, advancing the slogan _«All power to the General Council!»_. Revolutionary-minded groups, such as Guy Aldred’s Anti-Parliamentary Communist Federation, attempted to counter this with the slogan: _«NO power to the General Council! ALL POWER to Labour through its Strike Committees and Mass Meetings!»_ – but this appeal was published four days after the strike had been called off.

An episode from 1921 is particularly revealing in this regard: the secretary of the National Federation of Transport Workers who decided not to support the miners on “Black Friday” was Robert Williams, then a member of the CPGB. He was expelled from the Communist Party for this decision and subsequently returned to the Labour Party. The case of Williams raises a question that goes beyond individual betrayal: does a communist transform the trade union bureaucracy from within, or does the trade union bureaucracy transform the communist? Following the defeat of 1926, instead of drawing the conclusion that the old-style mass trade unions were structurally incompatible with the tasks of revolutionary struggle, the CPGB leadership demanded… an expansion of the powers of that very TUC General Council which had just betrayed the strike, and its further integration with the Labour Party.

The bureaucracy’s true attitude towards workers’ self-organisation on the eve of these events was expressed with striking brevity by J. R. Clynes, a member of the TUC General Council: “_I am not in fear of the capitalist class. The only class I fear is our own”_[^11].

The defeat of 1926 weighed heavily on the British miners’ movement, but it recovered: the victorious strikes of 1972 and 1974 demonstrated its renewed strength. Only the defeat of 1984-1985 broke it for a generation. It paved the way for anti-trade union legislation (the _Trade Disputes and Trade Unions Act_ 1927, repealed in 1946 and effectively revived under Thatcher) and entrenched the subordinate position of the trade unions _vis-à-vis_ the state.

## The United Front Turned Inside Out: Revolution and Counter-revolution in China (1925–1927)

The Asian theatre of class struggle during this period provides a different, but no less tragic, example. The May Thirtieth Movement of 1925, triggered by the shooting of an anti-imperialist demonstration in the Shanghai International Settlement, gave rise to the sixteen-month Hong Kong–Canton Strike – one of the longest mass strikes in world history, which severely disrupted economic life in the British colony of Hong Kong and the Anglo-French concession of Shamian. The young Communist Party of China (CPC), which since 1923 had been bound by the Comintern’s directive to join the Kuomintang as a faction within this bourgeois party, led the strike committee and secured the fulfilment of some of the workers’ demands. However, this organisational arrangement inherently entailed the dissolution of the proletariat’s class party within an inter-class bloc. This was not simply a “strategic error” on the part of the ECCI, but had a firm material basis: the USSR, isolated on the international stage, urgently needed a strong foreign-policy ally on its eastern borders to counterbalance Britain and Japan. From the standpoint of Moscow’s _state interests_, Chiang Kai-shek’s bourgeois-nationalist army appeared a far more reliable partner than the young Chinese proletariat. The class independence of the Chinese workers was sacrificed to the objective diplomatic and military needs of the Stalinist state.

The catastrophe of 1927 was neither a deviation from the united-front tactic nor an “extreme application” of an otherwise sound tactic – it was its logical consequence. A united front with non-communist organisations – whether reformist trade unions in Europe or bourgeois-nationalist parties in the colonies – cannot in principle serve as an instrument of proletarian revolution: it necessarily subordinates communists to alien leadership and alien interests, eroding the one boundary on which the revolutionary party rests – the class boundary. This was precisely the point made by the Italian Communist Left as early as 1921, when it rejected the united front as a return to the bankrupt pre-war tactics of Social Democracy. The same point was borne out by the Kuomintang on 12 April 1927. Led by Chiang Kai-shek, whose power had been consolidated precisely through the bloc that the CPC had helped to build, the Kuomintang turned its weapons against its erstwhile “allies”. In Shanghai, trade unions and organs of urban self-government under Communist control were crushed; more than a thousand people were arrested and around three hundred executed. Several hundred people were killed in the first few days alone, and over the following weeks of repression the death toll from the Kuomintang’s terror in Shanghai alone reached between 5,000 and 10,000. Repression also spread to Canton, Nanjing, Hangzhou and other cities.

The objective conditions – a highly concentrated, modern industrial proletariat that had emerged around foreign capital in a country characterised by deeply rooted feudal and patriarchal relations – generated explosive revolutionary energy, but did not negate or mitigate the operation of this law: a thin stratum of proletarians, placed by their own party under the leadership of the national bourgeoisie, was crushed precisely because it lacked an organisation of its own, independent of the bourgeois bloc.

The material basis of this contradiction was even more pronounced than in post-revolutionary Russia: a concentrated industrial proletariat existed only in narrow enclaves – treaty ports such as Shanghai and Guangzhou – surrounded by a country in which real power over most of the territory lay in the hands of rival militarist cliques, whilst the countryside remained shackled by semi-feudal land rents and was virtually untouched by either industrial capitalism or revolutionary agitation. It was precisely for this reason that the Kuomintang claimed the role of national unifier – a task that the small urban proletariat could not accomplish on its own. This made an alliance with the Kuomintang materially attractive, and not merely tactically expedient.

The Chinese catastrophe is one of the clearest historical confirmations of the thesis upheld by the Communist Left ever since its polemics against the very doctrine of the united front at the Third and Fourth Congresses of the Comintern: there can be no such thing as a “properly organised” alliance with the progressive national bourgeoisie in which the preservation of formal guarantees of party independence could save the day – agreeing to such an alliance in the first place already amounts to abandoning the class line. It is telling that even the criticism advanced by the Trotskyist International Left Opposition, which took shape under the influence of the Chinese tragedy, went no further than demanding “genuine guarantees” of the CPC’s independence within the bloc with the Kuomintang. In other words, it remained within the framework of the same erroneous premise: that a united front with a bourgeois party can, in principle, be properly organised. The defeat of 1927 was therefore not so much a failure of the Comintern’s particular leadership as a confirmation of the correctness of those who had insisted that a united front as such was impossible – from the very moment the CPC joined the Kuomintang in 1923.

## The Crash of 1929: The End of Stabilisation and the Radicalisation of the Bourgeoisie

The temporary, partial stabilisation of capitalism achieved in the 1920s at the cost of crushing the revolutionary vanguards gave way to a profound crisis as early as the autumn of 1929. The Great Depression was not merely a cyclical downturn, but a devastating structural crisis of the over-accumulation of capital. The global collapse in sales and the chain reaction of bankruptcies were manifestations of a deeper structural contradiction: the colossal increase in labour productivity and the rate of exploitation, driven by Fordism and Taylorism, led to an over-accumulation of capital for which no profitable outlet could be found and, at the same time – since the growth of exploitation structurally limits the share of the total product accruing to wage labour – to a contraction in the effective demand of the masses. Both developments are expressions of one and the same process: capital expands beyond the limits that it itself imposes on the realisation of the value it produces.

For the international labour movement, this collapse meant a radical change in the very conditions of struggle. The era of tactical concessions and reformist manoeuvring had come to an end. Faced with the threat of a catastrophic fall in the rate of profit, the bourgeoisie was forced to resort to radical measures to save the system. At one pole, in the United States, this led to an attempt to save capitalism through large-scale state regulation and the partial integration of the labour movement into the system (the New Deal). At the other, in the devastated countries of continental Europe, capital staked its hopes on the final destruction of bourgeois-democratic institutions and the physical elimination of all independent workers’ organisations through the establishment of fascist dictatorships. Under these conditions, the class struggles of the 1930s inevitably raised the question of power – a question that the labour movement was wholly unprepared to resolve.

## The German Tragedy: From “Socialist” Coalitions to the Triumph of Nazism (1921–1933)

Whilst in Italy the bourgeoisie crushed the labour movement through a direct violent assault – _squadristi_ terror followed by the physical elimination of all independent proletarian organisations – in Britain the defeat was inflicted by other means: the capitulation of the trade union bureaucracy, which surrendered the 1926 General Strike without securing a single concession, and the subsequent enshrinement of this defeat in law (the aforementioned _Trade Disputes and Trade Unions Act 1927_). In Germany, however – a country with the most powerful and best-organised proletariat in the world – the defeat took an even more convoluted and theoretically complex form. The blood shed in the suppression of the Spartacists by the Social Democratic government in 1919 permanently divided the German working class. From the outset, this was no accident, no “split among comrades”, but a dividing line between proletarian and bourgeois politics: as early as January 1919, the SPD was acting as an instrument of bourgeois state violence against the revolution, not as a misguided section of the labour movement. The following decade was marked by the systematic erosion of this original dividing line through the Comintern’s zigzags – first towards adventurism, then towards opportunist blocs. It was precisely this erosion, rather than the personal weaknesses of particular leaders, that led the proletariat to capitulate to Nazism without a single shot being fired by the workers’ organisations themselves.

Having lost its best cadres during the adventurist March Action of 1921, the KPD entered the subsequent period in the history of the German labour movement weakened and demoralised. The action had not been undertaken in response to an objective crisis, but on the basis of the “theory of the offensive” artificially imposed by the Comintern (through Béla Kun). There was neither a crisis at the top nor a comparable surge in mass activity in Germany in the spring of 1921: the uprising was provoked from above, against a relatively calm background, and was therefore doomed from the outset.

Formally speaking, a revolutionary situation developed in Germany in 1923, meeting all three classical criteria: the paralysis of the state apparatus following the fall of the Cuno government; hyperinflation, which wiped out savings and real wages; a wave of strikes; and the formation of “proletarian hundreds”. However, the cause of the defeat cannot be reduced to the indecisiveness of the KPD leadership, as Trotsky did in his article “The Lessons of October” (1924). The defeat was already inherent not in the events at Chemnitz on 21 October 1923[^12], but in the decisions of the Fourth Congress of the Comintern in 1922, and followed directly from the adoption of the slogan “workers’ and peasants’ government”. In the autumn of 1923, the Comintern supported the KPD’s entry into coalition governments in the German states of Saxony and Thuringia alongside the SPD. This strategy was intended to create the conditions for a revolutionary uprising, but ultimately ran up against the absence of nationwide preparedness for an uprising and intervention by the central authorities. The KPD itself had been paralysed by its own institutional commitment to legality long before anyone’s personal resolve was put to the test; the uprising, called off at the decisive moment, and the isolated Hamburg uprising, crushed in blood, were consequences, not the root cause. Where Trotsky sees a specific tactical error in the implementation of an otherwise sound strategy, we should see the natural outcome of the united-front strategy itself, tested here in practice for the first time and yielding its first bitter fruits.

By the time of the collapse of the Weimar Republic, the paralysis of the working class had reached its peak – and this time, on both sides of the split, it assumed a form bordering on political suicide. But to speak here of a symmetrical collapse of two “kindred” organisations is entirely wrong. The SPD and the trade unions under its control (the ADGB) did not “betray” the workers in a moment of weakness – they fulfilled precisely the function they had assumed as early as 1919: they staked everything on saving the bourgeois state at any cost, tolerating Brüning’s emergency decrees and relying on the police and the constitution to contain Hitler. The culmination of this legalism was the “Prussian coup” (_Preußenschlag_) of 20 July 1932: Reich Chancellor von Papen, by presidential decree, deposed the legitimate Social Democratic government of Prussia, headed by Otto Braun – and neither the SPD, nor the Prussian police, formally loyal to it, nor the paramilitary _Reichsbanner_ offered the slightest resistance.

The KPD, by contrast, although completely subordinate to the Stalinist leadership, remained – unlike the SPD – an organisation of proletarian origin, albeit one corrupted by opportunism. It is precisely for this reason that its collapse is particularly instructive. The «Third Period» line, adopted by the Sixth Congress of the Comintern in 1928 and radicalised by the Tenth Plenum of the ECCI in July 1929, branded the SPD “social-fascist” and identified it as the bourgeoisie’s principal social bulwark, asserting that victory over fascism was impossible without first defeating Social Democracy. In August 1931, acting on a direct Comintern directive, the KPD supported the referendum on the early dissolution of the Prussian Landtag, initiated by the “Steel Helmet” and the Nazis. Nazi stormtroopers were able to seize control of the streets unimpeded partly because, by the decisive years, the KPD had been deprived of its own paramilitary self-defence organisation: the Red Front Fighters’ League (_Roter Frontkämpferbund_, RFB) had been banned by the authorities as early as 1929. The slogan “After Hitler, it’s our turn” (attributed to KPD circles at the time, although its precise attribution remains disputed) expressed the genuine conviction of the Comintern apparatus.

Trotsky’s International Left Opposition had existed since 1930 and had produced programmatic texts on Germany, in which it systematically argued that the theory of “social-fascism” was a disastrous policy. However, the remedy proposed by Trotsky – a united front with the SPD involving practical military cooperation to physically defeat the stormtroopers (“march separately, strike together”) – rested on the same erroneous methodological premise as the strategy of 1923 that had led to the catastrophe: it continued to assume that the SPD, despite the betrayal of its leadership, remained a workers’ party with which an alliance was possible. But refuting the theory of “social-fascism” does not establish the legitimacy of a united front with a party that had demonstrated its bourgeois, rather than working-class, character as early as 1919, and subsequently confirmed it through its passive capitulation on 20 July 1932.

The German-Dutch Left, by contrast, regarded the KPD as a degenerated party from the early 1920s onwards. It called for direct action through grassroots workers’ unions (AAUD), rejecting both trade unions and parliaments. By the 1930s, however, this current had already been marginalised both physically and organisationally.

The Italian Left operated on two levels, which must not be confused. From 1928 onwards, following the foundation of the Left Fraction of the Communist Party of Italy in Pantin, near Paris, the journal Prometeo was published, carrying on an ongoing polemic against the line of the “Third Period”. However, the mature, definitive perspective, which categorically rejected both the “Third Period” and Trotsky’s call for a united front as two sides of the same error, was developed only retrospectively – in the journal Bilan, launched by the Italian Communist Left faction in Brussels in 1933 as a theoretical summing-up of the entire post-war revolutionary experience. For Bilan, the SPD was not a misguided section of the labour movement, but an organic part of the bourgeois state apparatus. To call for a united front with the executioners of the revolution in the name of «saving democracy» was to sow deadly illusions. Nazism and Weimar democracy were not opposites, but merely two different methods by which capital preserved its dictatorship. The German working class was defeated not because of a tactical error in 1932, nor because of the indecisiveness of individual leaders in 1923, but because, from 1921–1922 onwards, it allowed itself to be drawn first into alliances with Social Democracy, and then into a choice between bourgeois democracy and fascism – losing a measure of its political independence at each stage.

Special mention should also be made of the KPD-O, led by Brandler and Thalheimer, who had been expelled from the KPD in 1929. It advocated the tactic of the united front from an even more opportunist standpoint.

The catastrophe of the German proletariat marked the culmination of a series of defeats throughout the 1920s and into the early 1930s. What collapsed was not the “subjective factor” – not the will and determination of individual leaders – but the very architecture of the strategy that the Comintern had been constructing since 1921–1922: adventurism, followed by the united front and government coalitions, then the “Third Period”, and finally the complete transfer of the initiative into the hands of capital. In the next part of this article, we shall examine how this global shift in the balance of class forces affected Marxist theory, changed the socio-economic position of the working class, and gave rise to a new, no less tragic strategy of the Popular Fronts, whose final act was played out on the barricades of Spain.
