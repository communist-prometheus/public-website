**_The Anatomy of Global Alienation_**

# The Physiological Limit of Capital and the Tasks of the Communist Vanguard

**_Previously in this series of articles, we laid bare the political-economic anatomy of contemporary capitalism: how the system, seeking to mitigate the falling rate of profit, has constructed a planetary sweatshop conveyor belt, disguising it as the “gig economy”. Here we move from the economic base to the subjective factor. We examine how this merciless machine grinds down living labour power, driving the class towards physiological exhaustion, and how it produces false consciousness through atomisation and commodity fetishism. An awareness of these processes dictates a single historical way out: a break with reformism and the necessity of forging an independent vanguard for uncompromising class struggle._**

## Communist Revolution – The Only Limit to Exploitation

The spatial expansion of capital and digitalisation have enabled the bourgeoisie to slow the process of the falling rate of profit, but capitalism today is confronting a new insurmountable barrier – the physical exhaustion of the working class.

The _Employment-to-population ratio_ indicator published by the World Bank provides a quantitative basis for tracking this process. In developed countries, the ratio of the number of employed persons to the total population of the corresponding group is stagnating or falling. The OECD’s _Employment Outlook 2025_ sounds the alarm: by 2060, the ratio of people of retirement age to those of working age will rise to 67 per cent. Bourgeois institutions see this merely as a threat to GDP and demand that older people be kept in the workforce (through raising the retirement age), attempting to compensate for the falling rate of profit by extracting absolute surplus value.

Here we return once again to the theses of Mark Fisher, who demonstrated that the response to such overwhelming pressure is not necessarily class anger, but mass psychopathology. The bourgeoisie successfully carries out the “privatisation of stress”: the structural contradictions of capitalism (alienation, poverty, burnout at work) are imposed upon the worker as their own personal psychological problems, requiring medication or training rather than political struggle. Younger generations of proletarians are sinking into _depressive hedonia_ – they are depressed and unhappy _not because they are deprived of pleasures_, but because they are unable to stop consuming them in an attempt to escape reality.

The demographic shift mentioned in the previous issue of our journal[^1] is also profoundly polarised. While the West, the countries of the former USSR and East Asia are experiencing population ageing, the economies of Latin America, Asia and Africa are characterised by young populations, whose surplus leads to mass unemployment and involuntary global migration.

This surplus is not an accidental demographic anomaly. Capitalist penetration into the agricultural sector of countries with a lower organic composition of capital destroys traditional peasant agriculture, generating vast masses of what Marx called latent and stagnant surplus population. Latent surplus population consists of dispossessed peasants ready at any moment to swell the ranks of the urban proletariat, while stagnant surplus population consists of masses whose employment is extremely irregular and who occupy the very bottom of the economy.

Marx wrote:

_«This source of relative surplus population is thus constantly flowing. But the constant flow towards the towns presupposes, in the country itself, a constant latent surplus population, the extent of which becomes evident only when its channels of outlet open to exceptional width. The agricultural labourer is therefore reduced to the minimum of wages, and always stands with one foot already in the swamp of pauperism»_.[^2]

It is precisely the existence of this bottomless reservoir that enables TNCs to keep the wages of industrial workers in the “Global South” at, or even below, the physiological minimum.

Capitalism literally grinds down living labour power. As the economist Alan Krueger demonstrated in a major study for the US National Institutes of Health (NIH), the economy is witnessing a dramatic decline in labour-force participation among men of _prime age_. Displaced by capital from the industrial core, they are dropping out of the labour force in large numbers because of severe chronic illnesses. The progressive export of working schedules of 70-100 hours a week accelerates this process. The Japanese terms _karoshi_ (death from overwork caused by a heart attack or stroke) and _karōjisatsu_ (suicide caused by work-related stress) have now become a global reality and have long since moved beyond Asia. According to a joint report by the WHO and the ILO, working more than 55 hours a week increases the risk of stroke by 35 per cent, and in 2016, according to the report’s estimates, such a workload was the cause of around 745,000 deaths from stroke and ischaemic heart disease worldwide.

The tragedy of this exhaustion has already become part of contemporary proletarian literature. It is written in the blood of Xu Lizhi – a young worker at the Chinese mega-factory Foxconn, which assembles Apple products, who died by suicide in 2014 after jumping from the window of his dormitory because of unbearable working conditions. His final poems about a body _«swallowed by iron»_ and a youth left behind on the assembly line became a grim manifesto documenting the despair of an entire generation of industrial workers in the “Global South”.

While in the most advanced capitalist countries the bourgeoisie has shifted predominantly towards the extraction of relative surplus value (through the intensification of labour and technological development), in other sectors of the world market the destruction of the proletariat proceeds by the classical methods of the nineteenth century – through the extraction of absolute surplus value. Comparing the living conditions of the contemporary Asian worker with Friedrich Engels’s descriptions in _The Condition of the Working Class in England_ (1845), we find no fundamental differences. Engels documented 14-hour shifts, life in insanitary slums, widespread industrial injuries and the ruthless exploitation of women’s and children’s labour in the factories of Manchester. Today, official reports by international human-rights organisations and the UN confirm that tens of millions of garment workers in Bangladesh, textile workers in Cambodia and electronics assemblers in Shenzhen, China, find themselves in an entirely analogous position. The Rana Plaza disaster in 2013, which buried 1,134 people alive, the overwhelming majority of them workers in the garment industry, because contractors sought to save money on safety, and the installation of the notorious “anti-suicide nets” at Foxconn factories are direct manifestations of the universal law of capitalist accumulation. Even the much-vaunted high-tech “green’’ economy rests to a significant extent on cobalt extraction in the Democratic Republic of the Congo, where manual labour and child labour are used on a mass scale, enabling capital radically to cheapen the raw material.

Here a crucial truth is laid bare: what may appear to be the barbarism of the era of primitive accumulation (plunder, extra-economic coercion, slavery) is in fact a necessary outcome of the expanded reproduction of capital on a global scale. Capitalism has a continuous need for a colossal industrial reserve army of labour in order to devalue labour power and suppress class struggle. The contemporary “high-tech” capital of the developed countries could not physically function without this monstrous exploitation, which serves as the principal instrument for cheapening the elements of constant and variable capital. As Marx put it, _«capital comes dripping from head to foot, from every pore, with blood and dirt»[^3]_ – and today that blood and dirt have not disappeared. They have merely been distributed across the world market and hidden within logistical chains, while remaining the structural foundation of the capitalist mode of production.

The bourgeois sociology itself is compelled to register this collapse, though it hides it behind sterile corporate terminology. The global reports of the Gallup Institute (_Gallup State of the Global Workplace_) record year after year a record level of daily stress among workers and a colossal fall in what they call “engagement” (_quiet quitting_). According to _Gallup_, almost 60 % of workers are psychologically detached from their work _–_ a phenomenon cowardly dubbed by the bourgeois media “quiet quitting”. Analysts lament that these losses of productivity cost global GDP around 8.8 trillion dollars. But the Marxist lens allows us to see the true essence: these figures are the sociological expression of the total alienation of labour, fundamentally described by Marx. What the press calls “quiet quitting” is in reality a spontaneous form of production sabotage, and not a whim of the modern generation of wage-workers. It is that very state of which Marx wrote as early as 1844:

_«_\[…\] _labour is external to the worker, i.e., it does not belong to his intrinsic nature; that in his work, therefore, he does not affirm himself but denies himself, does not feel content but unhappy, does not develop freely his physical and mental energy but mortifies his body and ruins his mind. The worker therefore only feels himself outside his work, and in his work feels outside himself. He feels at home when he is not working, and when he is working he does not feel at home. His labour is therefore not voluntary, but coerced; it is forced labour. It is therefore not the satisfaction of a need; it is merely a means to satisfy needs external to it. Its alien character emerges clearly in the fact that as soon as no physical or other compulsion exists, labour is shunned like the plague»._[^4]

Psychological detachment and depression are the biological and spiritual self-defence of an organism reduced to an animal state. In consequence, the worker _«only feels himself freely active in his animal functions – eating, drinking, procreating, or at most in his dwelling and in dressing-up, etc.; and in his human functions he no longer feels himself to be anything but an animal. What is animal becomes human and what is human becomes animal».[^5]_

“Quiet quitting” is a spontaneous, passive form of class resistance of the exhausted proletariat, the biological and psychic self-defence of the organism in response to the ruthless extraction of absolute surplus value.

Bourgeois productivity studies paradoxically confirm the absurdity of this system: it has been proved that after 40-50 hours of labour a week the objective return per hour of labour falls precipitously, and a man working 70 hours produces exactly as much as in 50. Yet capital consciously embarks on this barbarism. The reason is simple: the hiring of each new employee (variable capital) requires additional outlays (training, insurance, taxes). It is more profitable for venture capitalists and investment bankers physically to burn one employee in the furnace of a 100-hour working week than to hire two. This is the most vivid illustration of how capital spits out proletarians who have become “unnecessary”, physically destroying the class that creates society’s wealth.

Bourgeois demography and health economics cynically interpret this crisis exclusively as a managerial problem of “population ageing” and the burden on budgets, offering purely technocratic solutions: the expansion of commercial medicine and a raising of the retirement age. For them, the wear and tear of the worker is merely a cost on the state’s balance sheet. The symptoms of this collapse are regularly covered in the output of the bourgeois mainstream media, which record unprecedented “labour shortage” and demographic catastrophe. Yet liberal analysts see in the physical shortage of hands merely the consequence of the regimes’ political errors or of temporary managerial miscalculations, wholly ignoring the systemic essence of the problem.

By contrast, we turn to the classical analysis of Friedrich Engels, given in his work _The Condition of the Working Class in England_ (1845), where he named this phenomenon with absolute precision _«social murder».[^6]_ Capitalism does not merely “underpay”: by means of stress, instability, overwork and a toxic social environment it systematically shortens the lives of proletarians. Marx in _Capital_ summed it up:

«_Capitalist production, therefore, develops technology, and the combining together of various processes into a social whole, only by sapping the original sources of all wealth – the soil and the labourer»._[^7]

The present global health crisis and demographic dead end are the direct consequence of the fact that capitalist exploitation constantly induces the physiological exhaustion of the human organism.

Capital, however, is incredibly flexible. If the workers “burn out”, capital can compensate for this by drawing in new masses (through migration), by radical automation (replacing burnt-out people with AI in the service sector) or by temporary concessions (Keynesianism). And this is yet another confirmation that, without the subjective factor (a revolutionary party), the objective crisis may last indefinitely, leading to barbarism, but not to the collapse of the system.

## The Material Roots of False Consciousness: Polarisation, Atomisation and War

These tectonic shifts radically deform the political consciousness of the proletariat, intensifying its fragmentation. The gap between the objective position of the working class (as a “class in itself”) and its subjective political consciousness (as a “class for itself”) is today enormous. The following material factors contribute to this:

1\. **Polarisation of the labour market**: ILO research records the spread across the global labour market of two models of change: “skills upgrading’’ and “polarisation”. What bourgeois institutions mildly call “polarisation” means, in the language of Marxism, a sharp erosion of the intermediate strata and a division of employment into a narrow, highly paid labour aristocracy (IT/finance) and an enormous mass of low-paid, service proletariat lucking any guarantees. This gap today impedes the development of a unified class consciousness: the upper strata associate themselves with the bourgeoisie, whilst the lower are tied to local sectors.

2\. **Spatial deconcentration and desocialisation**: capital purposefully constructs the labour process so as to destroy the _«sociality of production»_. The journal _Spectre_ provides an excellent example: in American higher education (almost 8 per cent of the labour force of American capitalism), administrations on a mass scale replace tenured professoriate with casualised “adjuncts”. Like Uber drivers, they receive a beggarly piece-rate payment per course, are deprived of guarantees and are isolated from colleagues. Capital shatters production into atoms, pre-emptively blocking the capacity for aggregation.

3\. **The illusion of “surrogate capital” and “hustle culture”**: the bourgeoisie has successfully integrated the upper strata of workers into the financial casino. As data from the _Hampton Institute_ show, as of 2020 more than half of Americans owned shares, whilst three quarters held pension accounts tied to the stock market. In the stratum from the 50th to the 90th percentile, the average volume of securities belonging to a single worker reaches $132,000. As the researchers themselves summarise, _«the idea that workers and capitalists always have opposite interests is harder to convey to Americans whose wealth depends on the success of capitalists_».

Here we observe what Lenin brilliantly defined as the material basis of opportunism – the formation of a _labour aristocracy_. Today capitalism has added to this the tying of the deferred value of labour power (pensions) to speculative finance capital.

_«The receipt of high monopoly profits by the capitalists in one of the numerous branches of industry, in one of the numerous countries, etc., makes it economically possible for them to bribe certain sections of the workers, and for a time a fairly considerable minority of them, and win them to the side of the bourgeoisie of a given industry or given nation against all the others. The intensification of antagonisms between imperialist nations for the division of the world increases this urge. And so there is created that bond between imperialism and opportunism, which revealed itself first and most clearly in Great Britain, owing to the fact that certain features of imperialist development were observable there much earlier than in other countries»._[^8]

In parallel, management imposes a destructive “hustle culture”, in which salaried employees are persuaded to think like “Olympic athletes”, whilst work-life balance is declared the way of those who allegedly “hate their job”. Capitalists cynically compare IT proletarians to the independent farmers of the past, ignoring the fact that the farmer worked for himself, whereas the modern _coder_ burns out his nervous system for the monetisation of AI for venture investors. Moreover, the supposed “consent” to overtime is often dictated not by enthusiasm, but by covert compulsion: fear of dismissal, competition in the market, or dependence on work visas. Herein lies the material basis of their loyalty.

4\. **Commodity fetishism and the ideology of lack of alternatives**: we live in an era in which capitalism is presented as the only possible, natural system. Moreover, bourgeois culture has developed a defence mechanism: capital itself produces and sells an anti-capitalist product (for example, films about evil corporations or about the oppressed condition of wage labourers). Capitalism itself performs for us the work of satisfying our own discontent, allowing us to safely consume protest in the cinema, so as to return submissively to the conveyor belt or the monitor the next day.

5\. **Loss of identity and the phantom of the “middle class”[^9]**: in the USA almost 90 per cent of people illusorily consider themselves “middle class”. The relative diminution of the factory core has led to the workers’ loss of a vision of themselves as the sole advanced, productive class. This historical frustration is actively channelled by populist politicians, who exploit the ideology of “deindustrialization” and offer the proletariat reactionary utopian solutions for returning the economy to its archaic forms, superseded by capital.

6\. **Cultural and spatial isolation**: the ubiquitous development of the tertiary spheres has led to the spatial dispersal of the proletariat. If in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries compact residence of workers in their own districts near the factories, with collective leisure, was typical, today atomisation has reached an unprecedented level.

7\. **Militarism and chauvinism**: the most important factor of demoralisation today is capitalism’s course towards general imperialist war. Finding itself in a deadlock of over-accumulation, the USA and other imperialist powers openly resort to militarism: from the intervention in Venezuela in January 2026 to the carnage in Iran and Lebanon, as well as the war in Ukraine, now in its fifth year.

Not one of the capitalist factions (whether the “democratic” West or the “authoritarian” East) carries within itself any progressive content. The ideology of “national defence” or “anti-fascism” is used by capital exclusively to drive the proletariat into the trenches of a fratricidal war. These conflicts are not random anomalies of political course, but a direct consequence of an intensification of the struggle for the redivision of markets between old hegemons and new imperialist centres of power. A world collision of predators over the control of raw materials and logistics is inevitable.

The costs of war are shifted onto the workers through inflation, whilst nationalism is stoked to control their consciousness, setting local workers against migrants (as evidenced by the tightened ICE raids in the USA, and in Russia by the police and nationalists of the “Russkaya Obshchina” (“Russian Community” )).

## The Only Alternative Is Struggle

Proceeding from this sociological cross-section, the academic left hastens to diagnose the death of the revolutionary subject. It sees only a defeated, atomised class and sinks into what Mark Fisher called “_reflexive impotence_” – we recognise the absurdity and injustice of capitalism, yet cynically believe that nothing can be done. But Marxism is not a sociology of defeat; it is a guide to action. Our chief task is to rupture the supposed naturalness of the present order and to counter-pose to capital a genuine revolutionary alternative.

Competition for orders in the app, the isolation of adjuncts in the departments, the hatred of migrants – all these are attributes of the capitalist “peace”. But the costs of capitalist barbarism itself push the workers onto the path of direct confrontation.

In 2026 we clearly see this potential. The economic consequences of the imperialist carnage in the Greater Middle East and the explosive growth of inflation provoked in April a colossal wave of mass strikes in India. In enormous industrial hubs such as Noida, tens of thousands of proletarians of the automotive and electronics industries, whom workers in digitalised employment and IT spontaneously joined, took to the streets, engaging in battles with the police and demanding a 35 % pay rise. The mass strike remains a mighty weapon of our class.

At the same time, the struggle of Canadian municipal “blue-collar” workers in Montreal proves another crucial thesis: the main internal enemy of resistance today are the official trade unions. In the era of developed imperialism, trade unions have definitively lost their original character as organs of class defence. They have become structurally integrated into the apparatus of the bourgeois state, becoming an institutional police over the labour force. Integrated into the state machine, they do everything to localise conflicts, to prevent them from growing into a general political strike, and to force the workers to submissively accept impoverishment. This counter-revolutionary, bureaucratic essence of the reformist trade unions, ready to make any compromise with capital for the sake of saving the system, was brilliantly ridiculed by Bertolt Brecht in the play _Saint Joan of the Stockyards_ (1929), where philanthropy and the legal trade unions serve merely as a screen for the bloody machinations of the Chicago meat monopolies. Consequently, the organisational task of the present is the creation of internationalist factory and platform committees, wholly independent of the official trade-union _nomenklatura_ and of the bourgeois state. The communist vanguard must use the very digital networks built by capital for surveillance in order to form the horizontal coordination of strikes (like those that took place in India in April). A welding-together of the industrial core of the proletariat with the masses of gig workers is necessary, where the latter act as the communicative circulatory system of protest.

The very same conditions of life and work which, outside periods of struggle, lead to the division of the proletariat into a mass of solitary individuals, at times of highest exacerbation push it to miracles of mass solidarity. This historical transition from a submissive, atomised crowd to a monolithic class conscious of its strength was monumentally captured in the painting of Giuseppe Pellizza da Volpedo in _The Fourth Estate_ (1901) and in the innovative cinema of Sergei Eisenstein (the film _Strike_, 1925), where the working class appears not as a passive victim for compassion, but as an irresistible, _collective force of history_. The proletariat of the time of world communist revolution has yet to become the collective force of history, and then artists and poets, awakened by this wave, will reflect in their work humanity’s entry into its true history.

The old mole of history is bespattered with blood and sweat, but it inexorably burrows its tunnel towards our freedom. In order to break through the thickness of contemporary alienation, we must clearly recognise objective reality: tectonic structural shifts, the spatial deconcentration of production, the development of the tertiary sector and the illusion of owning “surrogate capital” have generated the highest level of competition in history within the proletariat itself. Bourgeois sociology and the systemic “left” hasten to declare our class forever defeated, fragmented into millions of solitary individuals leading, in the apt expression of the young Engels, a daily capitalist _«battle of all against all»_.[^10]

Our answer must be unambiguous. Against the background of the total crisis of capitalism, the workers must bear no sacrifices for the sake of saving the “national economies” of a warring bourgeoisie. We are obliged to counter-pose to the instantaneous sociological view a genuinely Marxist, historical vision. We must look at the working class not only through the prism of what it is now, but above all see what it will historically have to become.

We are obliged to reforge the imposed competition into proletarian solidarity, to cast off trade-union reformism, to advance the slogans of revolutionary defeatism, and to forge our own, independent communist vanguard. As was declared in our _Manifesto_, these are the first and most necessary steps towards the building of a world communist party.

Here we must resolutely rely on the Leninist critique of “tailism”. We reject the naive belief that revolutionary consciousness will appear of itself out of purely economic, everyday struggle.

This party must become not merely a passive political compass growing out of the actual practical movement of the working class itself. It must not substitute itself for the class; it must become the expression of the working class overcoming, through primary associations, its internal differentiation. The party must be a disciplined fighting vanguard of the class, capable of organising it for the seizure of political power, the smashing of the bourgeois state machine and the establishment of the dictatorship of the proletariat.

Capitalism has left us no other choice: imperialist barbarism or world proletarian communist revolution. We do not intend to resemble the pessimists whose gaze is riveted only to the temporary triumph of capital. This exit is the only one, and with revolutionary passion we shall continue the battle for a classless society in the wake of the generations of previous fighters.
