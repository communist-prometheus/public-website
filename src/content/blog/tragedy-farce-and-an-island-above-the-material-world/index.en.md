**_Political Shells of Modern Imperialism_**

# Tragedy, Farce, and the Island Above the Material World

**_Modern politics increasingly resembles a shadow theatre, where the scale of the stage sets is meant to conceal the narrowness of political choice. Governments change, parliaments go through acute crises, and the streets of European cities regularly fill with protesters turning to the slogans and aesthetics of past revolutions. Yet behind the facade of political instability lies the striking durability of the dictatorship of capital: the state apparatus continues methodically to redistribute social wealth in favour of monopolies, the military-industrial complex, and finance capital, while the working class remains politically fragmented and deprived of influence over determining the course of society._**

**_In this article, we turn to the classic formula of tragedy and farce not for the sake of a literary metaphor, but to uncover the material foundations of the modern political forms of capitalism. Taking the deep political crisis and recent protests in France as an example, we examine why traditional reformism has lost its former material basis, and why parliamentary struggle, while remaining one of the forms of political activity, cannot serve as a strategy for the emancipation of the working class in the epoch of imperialism._**

**_The question today is not which government will prove to be the “lesser evil” for wage labourers, but how to overcome the fragmentation of protests and build an independent political organisation of the working class capable of moving from the struggle for partial concessions from the ruling class to the struggle for political power._**

Marx, echoing a thought of Hegel’s, wrote that all great world-historic facts and personages appear, so to speak, twice: the first time as tragedy, the second time as farce. This formula has long since turned into a commonplace ornament of political commentary. It is eagerly quoted even by those who believe that history moves thanks to the wisdom of statesmen, the cunning of spin doctors, and a felicitous choice of words in an election platform. Yet Marx was not speaking of a simple repetition of past political forms, nor claiming that every appeal to earlier symbols testifies to the poverty of ideas of a new movement. In _The Eighteenth Brumaire_, he shows that the bourgeois revolutions of the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries turned to images of antiquity, borrowing the names, slogans, and costumes of the Roman Republic. This was no accidental whim: historically new tasks did not yet possess their own established political language, and the images of the past helped people grasp the scale of what was happening and act accordingly. Behind those images stood a real transformation of social relations.

But historical form does not always retain its connection with the content it originally expressed. When old images and political roles continue to be reproduced after the historical conditions that gave rise to them have been exhausted, they turn into a shell behind which there is no longer a corresponding transformative content. It is precisely in this sense that the formula of tragedy and farce allows us to pose the question of the political repetitions of the nineteenth century. It does not pass sentence on modern movements, but demands that we ascertain which social forces stand behind their slogans and symbols, what tasks they are capable of solving, and what new content is maturing beneath the borrowed forms.

In the same work, Marx also formulated the fundamental condition of genuine historical creativity: the social revolution must draw its poetry not from the past, but from the future. Therefore, the question is not whether a movement makes use of the legacy of earlier battles, but whether that legacy becomes a means for solving new historical tasks or replaces an independent political programme. We will return to this question at the end of the article.

Today’s politics provides the richest material for such a test. Parliaments sit, governments change, parties declare one another a threat to democracy, and experts explain to the worker why his own needs do not fit within budgetary constraints. Some states proclaim the defence of freedom, others of national sovereignty, still others of social justice or civilisational values. All of them demand from the workers trust in the state, patience, and readiness to bear the costs. Yet when it comes to who disposes of production, investment, and the results of social labour, political diversity suddenly displays a striking restraint.

In Swift’s _Gulliver’s Travels_, on the island of Laputa live people so absorbed in abstractions that they have to be brought back to reality by blows to the face delivered by their servants. They are capable of discoursing on celestial bodies, yet fail to notice what is happening beneath their feet. In modern political life, the role of the Laputan sages is played by experts, strategists, consultants, and commentators who, with extraordinary solemnity, discuss approval ratings, coalitions, electoral technologies, and the fate of constitutional procedures, as though the economy existed solely to supply them with material for forecasts.

In Hobbes, the state appears as an artificial body – the Leviathan – created by human beings and possessing its own political might. But the modern Leviathan has one characteristic feature: it constantly explains to the workers that society cannot afford to satisfy their needs, and just as constantly finds the means to protect property, financial stability, military spending, and the bailout of systemically important enterprises.

On the streets of Paris, where demonstrators hurl stones at police shields and the police respond with tear gas, Swift’s metaphorical image acquires an almost literal expression. People below are trying to get through to a political power that increasingly speaks to them in the language of budget constraints, constitutional procedures, and the demands of financial markets. People sometimes take to the streets in the costumes and with the slogans of earlier generations, and in this the “sages” from Laputa see a _second edition_ of May 1968. Yet back then, for all its limitations, the student movement became one of the impulses toward a general strike that encompassed, by various estimates, between seven and ten million people. If, however, the present protest remains confined within school and university walls or limits itself to reproducing the symbolism of the past, its radical aesthetics will remain a farce. It will not reach the scale of the tragedy of the largest postwar workers’ movement, which, in the absence of a revolutionary party and a communist programme capable of linking the mass strike to the struggle for the political power of the proletariat, ended in defeat and the strengthening of the political positions of the bourgeoisie.

The chronology is well known. The general strike began on May 13. On May 25–27, the Pompidou government, the trade unions, and the employers agreed on the outcome of negotiations on Rue de Grenelle: the minimum wage (SMIG) rose by 35 %, other wages by roughly 10 %, and union sections were recognised in enterprises. The trade union leadership – above all the CGT, linked to the French Communist Party (PCF) – steered the movement toward this outcome and a return to work. However, at a number of factories, including Renault, the workers rejected the settlement brought back to them, and the strike continued. Then, on May 30, de Gaulle dissolved the National Assembly, and in the June elections the Gaullists won a solid majority. The workers achieved no small gains, yet all of them were concessions within the existing order: by one estimate, the increase in the SMIG directly affected only about 7 % of wage labourers, and the multi-million-strong strike did not grow into an independent political offensive of the proletariat. The state apparatus retained its power, capitalist relations remained untouched, and the energy of the mass mobilisation was dissipated among partial agreements, party strife, and a return to customary parliamentary politics.

The experience of May 1968 shows that a deep political crisis and a mass mobilisation of the working class do not in themselves eliminate the rule of the bourgeoisie. Even when the habitual mechanisms of governance malfunction and the state power of the bourgeoisie confronts mass resistance, class relations continue to reproduce themselves so long as the working class does not acquire the capacity to act independently upon the entire system of social relations. A political form can undergo a crisis, alter its methods of governance, and seek new grounds of legitimacy without touching the economic foundations of the rule of capital.

The question is not whether a connection exists between economics and politics. The question is who disposes of social wealth, whose interests the state protects, and what possibilities remain for the working class to act independently.

The political forms of capitalism are neither empty stage sets nor independent engines of history. They arise under definite material conditions, consolidate relations of domination, organise competition among capitals, regulate conflicts, and, when necessary, alter the very conditions of accumulation. They are capable of facilitating the development of some tendencies, braking others, and giving economic contradictions new forms. But they cannot abolish the relations on which the system is based.

That is precisely why it is not enough for communists to expose political illusions. It is necessary to understand why they are reproduced, whom they serve, and how to overcome them not in abstract theory, but in the class struggle.

## I. The State Does Not Hover Above Classes

Historical materialism does not claim that every political event can be explained by a single economic cause, the way a school problem is solved by plugging a number into a ready-made formula. It claims something else: social relations cannot be understood if they are examined outside the mode of production, class interests, and the material conditions in which people reproduce their lives. Yet this is not a declaration about the importance of material conditions that suffices merely to be uttered. It is a demand for a concrete accounting: which production, whose property, which class, which stage of capitalist development?

In the preface to _A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy_, Marx linked the totality of legal and political relations to the material conditions of social life. Engels, replying to vulgar interpretations of this thesis, repeatedly emphasised the relative independence of political, legal, and ideological forms, as well as their reverse effect upon economic development. The fact that the economy determines history does not mean that the state is a puppet whose strings capitalists pull from behind the scenes. It means that the nature of the state cannot be explained while forgetting the relations of property and production that it is called upon to regulate and reproduce.

In _The German Ideology_, Marx and Engels formulated the starting principle of the materialist conception of history:

_«That is to say, not of setting out from what men say, imagine, conceive, nor from men as narrated, thought of, imagined, conceived, in order to arrive at men in the flesh; but setting out from real, active men, and on the basis of their real life-process demonstrating the development of the ideological reflexes and echoes of this life-process. The phantoms formed in the brains of men are also, necessarily, sublimates of their material life-process, which is empirically verifiable and bound to material premises. Morality, religion, metaphysics, and all the rest of ideology as well as the forms of consciousness corresponding to these, thus no longer retain the semblance of independence. They have no history, no development; but men, developing their material production and their material intercourse, alter, along with this their actual world, also their thinking and the products of their thinking. It is not consciousness that determines life, but life that determines consciousness»[^1]._

This is not a call to ignore ideas and political convictions. On the contrary, Marxism demands an explanation of the social relations from which they arise, why certain of them acquire a dominant position, and what role they play in reproducing the existing order.

In _The Eighteenth Brumaire of Louis Bonaparte_, Marx returns to this principle, now applying it to the analysis of concrete political struggle. He shows how, on the basis of different forms of property and social conditions of existence, a class’s characteristic sentiments, illusions, modes of thought, and views of life take shape. Political conceptions are treated here neither as a simple reflection of the economy nor as an independent force capable of arbitrarily determining the course of history: Marx investigates their connection with the material position of classes, their interests, and their practical struggle. Therefore, philosophical reasoning about the state divorced from the study of production, property, and class relations risks turning into the very occupation that Marx and Engels criticised in _The German Ideology_: explaining the real world through ideas about it taken as the independent cause of historical events.

The capitalist state is not obliged to take orders from every individual entrepreneur. Moreover, it can enter into conflict with individual capitalists, raise taxes, restrict certain transactions, nationalise loss-making enterprises, or compel business to comply with rules disadvantageous to it at a given moment. But it does not follow from this that the state ceases to be the state of capitalist society.

A factory manager can also conflict with an individual shareholder, fire an inconvenient executive, and alter the production plan. It would be strange to conclude on that basis that the factory has stopped producing commodities for the sake of capital accumulation.

The state ensures the general conditions for the reproduction of capitalist relations: legal protection of property, monetary circulation, infrastructure, enforcement of contracts, training of the workforce, management of social conflicts, and defence of national capital against external encroachments. It does not merely service already established interests. It itself organises them, weighs competing demands of capitalist groups, sets priorities, and distributes risks.

Therefore, state policy cannot be reduced to the immediate wishes of the most influential entrepreneur. Capitalist society does not consist of a single capital with a unified headquarters, but of a multitude of capitals competing with one another while simultaneously depending on the existence of general conditions of accumulation. The state acts as the political organiser of these general conditions without ceasing to be part of class society.

Engels called the modern state the ideal collective capitalist. It is not the servant of each individual entrepreneur, but an organisation that represents capital as a whole, including against its private representatives. Yet Engels’s emphasis falls on exploitation, not solely on organisation: the more productive forces the state takes into its ownership, the more it becomes an actual collective capitalist and the more citizens it exploits. Workers remain wage labourers, and the capitalist relation is not abolished but pushed to its extreme. Consequently, the nationalisation of a loss-making enterprise does not take the state outside class boundaries, and organising the general conditions of accumulation often means shifting risks onto wage labourers.

In _The Origin of the Family, Private Property and the State_, Engels defined the state as a power that appears to stand above society: it arises because society has split into irreconcilable classes, and it is called upon to moderate their collision so that the classes do not consume themselves and society in fruitless struggle. Two qualifications to this definition are crucial. First, there are periods when the warring classes balance each other so nearly that state power temporarily acquires that very semblance of independence from them. Second, capital exercises its power not only directly, but also indirectly: through the corruption of officials and an alliance between the government and the stock exchange – and in a democratic republic this is done with particular freedom, since formal distinctions of property carry no legal weight there. These remarks explain two things at once: why the state is capable of conflicting with capitalists, and why that conflict does not take it outside class boundaries.

It is precisely here that the image of the Leviathan requires clarification. The state indeed possesses its own organisation, apparatus of coercion, and interests that do not coincide with the immediate interests of every individual owner. It can discipline entrepreneurs, redistribute resources between sectors, restrict some fractions of capital for the sake of others, and even temporarily enter into conflict with influential representatives of the bourgeoisie. But this relative independence does not turn it into a force standing above class society. The Leviathan does not float in a vacuum: its political might rests upon definite property relations, and its decisions ultimately reproduce the conditions for the existence of those relations.

Here too begins the problem of bourgeois “democracy”.

Formal political equality of citizens is entirely compatible with profound inequality in their real social power. A worker and the owner of large capital may possess the same right to vote. Yet one of them sells his labour power, while the other makes decisions about capital investment, plant closures, the relocation of production, and the fate of thousands of jobs.

At the polling station they are equal. In production, they are not.

It is precisely this distinction that bourgeois ideology prefers to treat as secondary. Political “democracy” is presented as the consummate expression of popular sovereignty, even though a substantial share of the decisions shaping the life of society is made outside the direct control of the majority. Voters choose representatives, but do not determine investment policy. Citizens possess constitutional rights, but do not thereby gain the right to dispose of the enterprise where they work. The population can change a government, but cannot with a single vote abolish the dependence of its life on the accumulation of capital.

“Democracy” in capitalist society is not simply a deception. It creates real possibilities for organisation, struggle, public criticism, and winning concessions. That is precisely why those possibilities must be fought for. But it does not eliminate class power based on property and control over production.

The liberal illusion begins where political equality is mistaken for social equality, and the ability to change a government for the ability to change the relations that reproduce the power of capital. There is also a left-wing variety of this illusion: the belief that it suffices to defend the constitution from abuses, or replace it with a fairer one, for the constitution itself to become a guarantee. A constitution does not hover above class conflicts. It is the supreme law and absolute will of the ruling class – its own _Bible_, in which a convenient verse has been prepared in advance for every political occasion.

## II. From Machiavelli to Lenin: Political Form and Class Content

The history of political thought has repeatedly uncovered the contradiction between the official principles of the state and the actual conditions of its existence.

Machiavelli investigated political power not as an embodiment of virtue, but as a distinct practice subordinated to the struggle for its acquisition and preservation. He was interested in the effectual truth of things, not pious notions of what rulers ought to be. Hobbes viewed the state as an artificial order designed to end destructive conflict and ensure security. These approaches freed political analysis from the naive belief that rulers act solely out of a love of justice.

Marx took the next step: he linked political forms to historically definite social relations and the class struggle.

In _The Eighteenth Brumaire of Louis Bonaparte_, he showed how the state apparatus can acquire relative independence, elevate itself above the directly contending classes, and simultaneously preserve the conditions of existence of bourgeois society. Political power does not always appear in the form of the direct and transparent rule of individual capitalists. It can present itself as the embodiment of the nation, public order, the popular will, or the interests of all citizens.

Bonapartism was important precisely because it demonstrated that political form is capable of changing, manoeuvring between social forces, and subordinating their immediate representatives without destroying the social relations that make such power possible.

In _The Eighteenth Brumaire_, Marx examined not only the personal adventure of Louis Bonaparte, but the political mechanics of a society in which the bourgeoisie, in order to preserve its social domination, proves ready to sacrifice direct control over political power. The state apparatus takes on the appearance of an independent force promising to protect society from chaos, party squabbles, and class conflict. Yet this independence has a quite definite class function. Marx expressed the paradox by saying that in order to preserve its social power, the bourgeoisie had to break its own political power, and in order to save its purse, it had to forfeit the crown.

Hence Marx’s famous conclusion: all revolutions perfected this machine instead of smashing it. He did not mean that all reforms are useless or that all political struggle is doomed in advance. He meant the problem of the state apparatus itself, shaped under the conditions of class society.

This conclusion did not remain abstract. During the Paris Commune, Marx wrote to Kugelmann (April 1871) that the next attempt of the French revolution must no longer consist in transferring the bureaucratic-military machine from one set of hands to another, but in smashing it, and saw in the Commune a heroic attempt at such an undertaking. In _The Civil War in France_, he described what replaced that apparatus: officials are elected, accountable, and recallable, receiving the average wage of a worker; the standing army gives way to the armed people; legislative and executive functions are combined. Engels, in his 1891 introduction to that work, explicitly called the Commune the dictatorship of the proletariat and reminded readers that the state remains a machine for the oppression of one class by another in a democratic republic no less than in a monarchy: the victorious proletariat will inherit this evil and lop off its worst sides until a new generation is able to throw the entire lumber of the state on the scrap heap.

Lenin, in _The State and Revolution_, quotes Engels’s definition of the state and places an emphasis that must not be lost: the state is an organ of class rule, an organ for the oppression of one class by another; it is the creation of “order”, which legalises and perpetuates this oppression by moderating the collision between classes. Petty-bourgeois politicians read “moderate” as “reconcile” and see in the state an organ for the reconciliation of classes. Lenin objects: moderate – yes, but in the interests of the ruling class. The state only appears to be an arbiter; in reality, it is a force entrusted with guarding the order needed by the ruling class.

In the same work, developing the thought of Marx and Engels, Lenin called the democratic republic the best possible political shell for capitalism: having gained possession of it, capital establishes its power so securely that no change of persons, institutions, or parties can shake it. This thesis must not be turned into an eternal law, as if the bourgeoisie were obliged under all circumstances to prefer parliamentary democracy. The political shell is determined not by the aesthetic preferences of the ruling class, but by historical conditions, the balance of forces, and the requirements of the system’s reproduction.

Here a qualification in the spirit of Engels is needed. The democratic republic is not only a shell, but also an arena on which the class struggle is fought through to the end. Hence the twofold attitude of communists toward the legal arena: neither renouncing it nor believing that it can resolve the question of power.

Lenin stated clearly and definitively what followed from the context of Marx and Engels: every state is the dictatorship of the ruling class, and the democratic republic is merely its best form. Therefore, the alternative to the democratic shell is not a more “genuine” democracy, but a state of a new type: the dictatorship of the proletariat, organised on the model of the Commune and the Soviets – that is, the power of the armed working majority, suppressing the resistance of the exploiters and initiating the withering away of the state itself.

Capitalism itself lays the material premise for the dictatorship of the proletariat. Banks, monopolies, energy and transport networks are an already prepared apparatus of accounting and distribution, socialised in form and private in appropriation. The withering away of the state is therefore not a wish, but a consequence of the socialisation prepared by capitalism. After the smashing of the bourgeois state apparatus, as accounting and control become the affair of all rather than of a special stratum, the need for an apparatus of violence standing above society will also disappear. These are measures of proletarian power, not demands addressed to the bourgeois state.

Yet the example of the Commune demands communist sobriety. The Commune was defeated, and its defeat was tied not only to the superiority of the enemy, but also to its own political and organisational mistakes. In his letter to Kugelmann of April 12, 1871, Marx wrote that the Parisians should have marched on Versailles immediately after the retreat of the government troops and the reactionary part of the National Guard: the moment was missed out of scrupulousness and unwillingness to be the first to start a civil war. In his letter to Wilhelm Liebknecht of April 6, he specifically pointed out that the desire not to appear as usurpers of power led to the loss of precious time, including by waiting for the Commune elections. Nor did the Communards take control of the French central bank, which could have become a vital means of pressure on Versailles.

The political leadership of the Commune was likewise not homogeneous. Various currents existed within it: Neo-Jacobins, Blanquists, supporters of the International, and adherents of other socialist tendencies. The split over the creation of the Committee of Public Safety in May 1871 revealed the depth of these disagreements and led to the withdrawal of part of the minority from the Commune. The causes of defeat, of course, cannot be reduced to internal errors: Paris found itself isolated, provincial communes were crushed, and Bismarck granted Versailles the opportunity to use released French prisoners of war to reinforce the government army.

The lesson, however, is clear. The institutions of the Commune – the election and recall of officials, the average worker’s wage, the replacement of the standing army by the armed people – marked out a fundamentally new form of political power. But institutions without force do not hold. This applies to any political form, including the one we intend to build.

The same law operates for the bourgeois state. In the final analysis, the bourgeois state rests not on parliament, but on the armed force it already possesses. Parliament is a way of governing as long as it suffices; the army and police are the guarantee of power when it does not. Therefore, the form of power changes as necessity dictates: as long as parliamentary procedures cope, force remains behind the scenes, and when they cease to cope, it steps to the fore.

Where parliamentary procedures help organise the rule of capital, ensure changes of government, and channel social conflicts within manageable bounds, they prove a convenient instrument. Where crisis, war, international competition, or acute class struggle make customary procedures inconvenient, the state can strengthen the executive, expand emergency powers, restrict protests, and shift decisions into structures less accountable to society.

This does not necessarily mean an immediate transition to dictatorship. Between stable parliamentary democracy and open terror there exist many intermediate forms. Yet no constitutional construction frees society from the question of who holds real power over production and state coercion.

The bourgeoisie is not noted for consistency in matters of political philosophy. When democratic procedures ensure governability, it extols them as the highest achievement of civilisation. When they begin to interfere with carrying out the necessary policy, it turns out that the constitution permits exceptions, standing orders contain fast-track procedures, and experts prepare an explanation for why this time they cannot be done without.

Principles are declared eternal right up to the very moment when the need arises to depart from them.

## III. The Imperialist Stage: What Changes in the Shell?

Lenin defined imperialism as the monopoly stage of capitalism: the dominance of monopolies and finance capital, the predominance of the export of capital over the export of commodities, the division of the world among capitalist combines and among the great powers. As the political consequence of this economy, he saw not the flowering of democracy, but – in his 1916 formula (_Imperialism and the Split in Socialism_) – a striving toward violence and reaction. The shell of the imperialist epoch therefore differs from the shell of the epoch of free competition: banks fuse with industry and the state apparatus, key decisions are concentrated in a narrow circle of the executive, and militarisation and pressure on labour grow. War, as Lenin wrote in 1917, accelerates the transformation of monopoly capitalism into state-monopoly capitalism.

Before turning to the French political shell, we must lay bare its material foundation. France in recent decades is a showcase of the restructuring of the industrial apparatus and the triumph of finance capital: industrial production departs, the rate of profit is sustained by pressure on wages and share buybacks, and social wealth shifts from wages to dividends. In 2025, CAC 40 companies paid shareholders a record €107.5 billion: €72.8 billion in dividends and €34.8 billion in share buybacks (roughly 68% and 32%). For the first time, payouts exceeded €100 billion, and records are broken year after year. At the same time, the very same country remains the world’s second-largest arms exporter: according to SIPRI, it accounted for 9.8 % of global deliveries in 2021–2025, 21 % more than in the preceding five years. Foreign orders in 2025 totalled €21.2 billion, of which roughly 43 % came from Asia and roughly 27 % from European Union countries. These are not two different countries, but a single economic configuration in which the large shareholder and the defence contractor occupy a privileged place among recipients of the social product, while schools, hospitals, and the worker’s wage become residual budget items. What follows is the story of how the state apparatus services this construction and along which seams it is beginning to crack.

The state acts simultaneously as arms buyer, organiser of domestic "fiscal consolidation," and debtor through which finance capital receives first claim on budget revenues. Debt itself is not a hallmark of imperialism, but a consequence of accumulated deficits, crises, and wars: Marx saw in public debt a lever of accumulation already in the era of primitive accumulation, and imperialism merely makes it permanent. For the shell, something else matters: debt service is entrenched as an obligation standing ahead of funding for schools and hospitals. According to figures released in July 2026, debt service costs will rise from €64.8 billion in 2026 to €74.2 billion in 2027 (around 26 % of its budget), and military spending by another €6.4 billion under the military programming law. In April 2026, the defence minister announced a revision of that law: the defence budget will increase by €36 billion over four years, reaching €63.3 billion in 2027 and €76.3 billion by the end of the decade. Simultaneously, the Lecornu government is seeking to reduce the deficit to 5 % of GDP in 2027 through a €54 billion adjustment, of which €43 billion consists of new measures. The education budget, according to the prime minister, will grow by €1.2 billion.

These figures should not be read as a simple “guns instead of schools”: a budget is not a cash register with two windows, and the growth in interest payments is determined by accumulated debt. Yet they do show the order of priorities. Spending on the coercive apparatus and debt service is locked in by multi-year legislation and external obligations, whereas schools, hospitals, and wages remain the subject of an annual bargaining process in which they are the first to be declared an extravagance.

The rivalry of the powers for markets, raw materials, and spheres of influence – placed by Lenin at the foundation of the definition of imperialism – is carried out by armed force, and France is a participant in it, not a bystander. Rearmament of €36 billion over four years is simultaneously insurance and preparation: each state arms itself “for defence”, and for that very reason the arms race pushes toward collision. For workers, the conclusion follows that the Bolsheviks formulated back in 1914: in an imperialist war, they have no fatherland to defend.

## IV. The Parliamentary Spectacle and the Exhaustion of Reformism

Parliamentary life turns real social contradictions into a contest among political representatives. Instead of discussing property relations, citizens are offered a choice between parties arguing over tax rates, budget priorities, the pace of reforms, and how to distribute the costs of crisis.

These differences can have immediate consequences. Yet their significance cannot be assessed outside the historical conditions in which the political system operates.

The social compromise that took shape after the Second World War was not an eternal property of capitalism. It rested on concrete conditions: the reconstruction of devastated economies, rapid productivity growth, possibilities for expanded accumulation, and the redistribution of part of the value produced. In a number of countries, it was combined with mass trade union organisations and substantial social concessions. Yet those concessions did not abolish exploitation and did not signify a movement toward the emancipation of labour. They were a mode of reproducing capitalist relations in a definite historical period.

The wave of 1968–1969 also reveals the other side of the postwar social compromise. In Italy, the “Hot Autumn” of 1969 brought workers new collective agreements, and in May 1970 the Workers’ Statute was adopted, enshrining important rights for workers and trade union organisations in enterprises. These were conquests of the class struggle, not the generosity of the bourgeoisie. Subsequently, the bourgeoisie undertook sustained efforts to curtail these guarantees. The 2012 labour legislation reform altered the operation of Article 18 of the Statute, restricting cases of a worker’s reinstatement in his former job after unlawful dismissal. The 2015 legislation known as the Jobs Act restricted the application of reinstatement even further for new employment contracts, expanding the scope of monetary compensation. These changes did not abolish the Statute in its entirety, but substantially weakened one of its most important guarantees. Concessions not backed by organisation and a durable balance of class forces can be curtailed or taken back, even if the workers’ movement continues to resist.

Capital’s offensive against wages, labour guarantees, and public spending was organised by the state through legislation, fiscal policy, policing measures, and the restructuring of labour relations. The weakening of independent class organisation made it easier to carry out this policy.

Here it is necessary to turn directly to Engels’s 1895 introduction to Marx’s _The Class Struggles in France_, 1848 to 1850. It was precisely this text that later became one of the chief theoretical foundations for the legal and parliamentary strategy of Social Democracy. Engels wrote that the era of surprise attacks and street barricade battles was passing, that universal suffrage opened new possibilities of struggle for the working class, and that the bourgeoisie and the government had come to fear the legal action of the working class more than its illegal action. He proceeded from concrete changes in military affairs, urban organisation, and the numbers and political organisation of the proletariat. Later, the Social Democratic tradition turned these observations into a justification for an ever broader abandonment of the revolutionary perspective. Yet Engels himself renounced neither the necessity of the revolutionary transformation of the state nor the dictatorship of the proletariat.

Historical conditions, however, do not remain unchanged. At the end of the nineteenth century, the expansion of the industrial proletariat and its organisations created one configuration of the class struggle. The growth of productivity and capital accumulation after the Second World War made it possible to consolidate another: mass industrial employment, strong trade union organisations, and a prolonged social compromise in a number of developed capitalist countries. Subsequent restructuring of production, relocation of industrial capacity, pressure on wages and public spending, weakening of trade unions, and the reproduction of privileged strata of the working class on the basis of imperialist super-profits altered that configuration. Reformism did not merely suffer a political defeat: its former material basis was substantially undermined. Legal struggle remains a necessary field of class activity today, but it no longer follows from this that expanding parliamentary representation and social concessions opens a stable path toward the gradual transformation of capitalism.

Under these conditions, one cannot simply continue the old programme, as though it were enough to return a more benevolent government to power, restore the former balance of parliamentary forces, and once again seat capital at the negotiating table with the trade unions.

The exhaustion of reformism as a historical strategy does not mean that the state is no longer capable of making concessions. It means that isolated concessions cannot be taken as the basis for a stable path toward the gradual transformation of capitalism in the interests of the working class.

The state can cancel a specific cut, increase funding for a particular sector, amend labour legislation, or make concessions under pressure from a mass workers’ movement. But such decisions depend on the concrete balance of forces and can be reversed when it shifts. They do not indicate a restoration of the former conditions of social compromise and do not by themselves create a long-term tendency toward improving the condition of the working class.

Reformism turns the partial results of struggle into a strategy: it proposes achieving the gradual transformation of society through the successive expansion of social guarantees, parliamentary representation, and negotiations with the state. When the material conditions for such a movement are undermined, preserving this strategy means not realism, but a refusal to recognise a changed historical situation.

Why, then, is the "lesser evil" so tenacious? The answer cannot be reduced to the claim that workers are deceived. And the analysis of the problem here must begin with those who continue to act under the communist banner. The history of the PCF is one of the most telling examples of the Stalinisation of the international workers’ movement. Emerging in 1920 on the wave of revolutionary upsurge, it was in subsequent years subordinated to Stalinist policy, turning from an organisation that claimed leadership of the revolutionary struggle into an apparatus for the political subordination of the working class to the interests of preserving the capitalist order. Under a red banner, it began to pursue a policy that time and again diverted the workers’ movement from the struggle for power toward parliamentary combinations, class collaboration, and defence of the state.

This is no accidental departure from the communist programme and no mistaken tactic of individual leaders. Lenin, developing the observations of Marx and Engels, investigated the material conditions for the spread of opportunism in the workers’ movement. The super-profits obtained by imperialist powers through their monopoly position and the exploitation of dependent countries make it possible to maintain a privileged position for certain strata of the working class and create social soil for conciliationism. Back in 1858, Engels wrote to Marx about the growing bourgeoisification of the English proletariat. Lenin showed how imperialism reproduces a labour aristocracy and strengthens opportunist currents.

However, opportunism cannot be reduced to the influence of the labour aristocracy, nor can the class nature of individual parties be explained by it alone. Opportunism is bourgeois influence within the workers’ movement; its sources are broader, and it is consolidated organisationally: in parties, trade union apparatuses, and other institutions that reproduce the policy of class collaboration. Therefore, the PCF and organisations like it should be viewed not as a simple political expression of privileged strata of the working class, but as red-flagged bourgeois parties whose class role is determined by their programme, strategy, and practical attitude toward the state and the capitalist order.

The PCF must be viewed precisely in this historical context. Its role in the Popular Front, the postwar restoration of the bourgeois order, and the events of May–June 1968 shows that these were no accidental compromises. At moments when the sweep of mass struggle created an objective possibility of going beyond capitalist relations, the party leadership strove to keep it within the bounds of economic demands, negotiations, and the parliamentary perspective. That is precisely why the PCF must be judged not by its name, the historical merits of particular generations of communists, or its red symbolism, but by its actual class function.

The same test is necessary with respect to other forces claiming leadership of the present movement. The French Socialist Party (PS), La France insoumise (LFI), and other parliamentary organisations of the left opposition offer different variants for managing the bourgeois state, not an independent policy of the working class. The trade unions play the role of a straitjacket on workers’ protests and a transmission belt for parliamentary parties. These organisations cannot be lumped into a single category; on the contrary, it is necessary to establish the exact function each of them performs in subordinating the workers’ movement to the existing political order.

Laputans exist in the workers’ movement as well. They are those accustomed to translating every grievance into the language of negotiations, standing orders, and electoral calculation – not out of stupidity, but because it is their trade. Expertise here is not stupidity, but a social position. Parliamentary cretinism manifests itself not even in participation in elections or parliamentary activity as such. It manifests itself in turning parliamentary procedures into the sole horizon of political activity, in the conviction that the right combination of deputies and ministers can replace an independent movement of the working class.

Under this approach, workers are turned into an electorate periodically urged to support the “lesser evil”. After the elections, they are told to show patience so as not to hinder the fight against an even greater evil. A few years later, it turns out that the lesser evil has become the customary mode of governance, and workers are once again urged to vote for it – now to save “democracy” from the consequences of the previous salvation.

In Saltykov-Shchedrin’s _The History of a Town_, the activity of Mayor Brudasty was reduced to a mechanical barrel organ capable of reproducing two commands: “I will not tolerate it!” and “I will ruin you!” The modern political machine is, of course, more complex than Shchedrin’s mechanism. It has parties, parliamentary committees, expert councils, polling agencies, and an entire industry of political commentary. Yet in moments of crisis, its repertoire becomes laughably predictable: responsibility, stability, the necessity of reforms, the absence of alternatives, national interests, an emergency situation. The announcers, backdrops, and party colours change; the vocabulary remains.

This does not mean that all parliamentary parties are identical in every respect or that political differences do not matter at all. But differences between ways of managing capitalism do not imply a change in the strategy for the emancipation of the working class.

The choice before the working class does not come down to deciding which political camp will provide it with a less painful “organised retreat”. Before it stands a more fundamental alternative: to capitulate to the offensive of capital or to resist it; to remain an atomised mass of wage labourers or to build its own organisations; to follow bourgeois parties or to win over ever broader strata of working people by developing an independent class policy.

It is precisely here that the political task of the world communist party comes into view. It cannot limit itself to stating that the former social compromise has lost its material basis and on those grounds leave the working class to its own fate. One cannot fight to overcome capitalism while writing off in advance comrades of one’s own class who do not yet share the communist programme today. If communists do not participate in their everyday conflicts, do not help them organise, and do not prove in practice the necessity of an independent class policy, that work is done by other political forces – in the interest of preserving the existing order or restructuring it on new, yet still bourgeois, foundations.

The struggle for wages, working conditions, social needs, and the right to organise does not become pointless just because capitalism no longer offers the former possibilities for a durable reformist compromise. On the contrary, amid a capitalist offensive, abandoning such a struggle would mean voluntarily ceding to the adversary the field on which political experience, solidarity, and the capacity for collective action are forged. An isolated concession does not restore the historical perspective of reformism, but the struggle for it can become part of a process in which workers learn to resist, unite, and recognise the commonality of their interests.

Therefore, the question is not how to reconcile oneself to an inevitable retreat, but how to halt it, pass over to a counteroffensive, and turn fragmented resistance into an independent class movement. Its outcome is not predetermined: it depends on the balance of forces, the level of organisation, political experience, and the ability of communists to connect immediate demands with the perspective of overcoming capitalist relations.

Rejecting reformism does not mean rejecting the winning of even the most minor partial improvements. It is a refusal to regard their improbable accumulation as a guaranteed path to the emancipation of labour. The world communist party must fight for every opportunity to strengthen the positions of the working class, without passing off a limited result as the final goal and without turning defence of the existing state of affairs into a strategy. Its task is not to manage an “organised retreat”, but to help transform resistance into a struggle to change the very balance of class forces and, ultimately, to abolish the social relations that make exploitation and capitalist rule possible.

The world communist party must not invite workers to entrust their fate to the most benevolent manager. Its task is to develop an independent class organisation that does not hand over its will to parliamentary intermediaries and does not reduce political activity to pressuring the state for the next deal.

In this sense, criticism of the parliamentary spectacle must not turn into yet another spectacle – even one with red stage sets. It is not enough to expose deputies, ministers, and union intermediaries for workers to gain their own political strength. Such strength arises not from having the right attitude toward parliament or trade unions, but from the practice of joint struggle, in which workers learn to make decisions, elect representatives, hold them accountable, and defend common interests. Communists must be participants in this process, not spectators explaining from the balcony to indignant proletarian masses their historical inadequacy.

## V. France: State Concessions and the Limits of the Reformist Perspective

France vividly illustrates how a crisis of political form arises within functioning institutions of bourgeois “democracy2.

The dissolution of the National Assembly in June 2024 led to political fragmentation and made it difficult to form a stable parliamentary majority. Governments succeeded one another, budget decisions turned into the subject of protracted conflict, and the executive resorted to constitutional instruments allowing legislative initiatives to be pushed through without a standard vote on the merits, provided the government survived a vote of no confidence.

The Bayrou government fell in September 2025 after failing to secure approval for a budget with €44 billion in cuts; Sébastien Lecornu, appointed on September 9, survived two no-confidence votes in October only because he suspended the pension reform raising the retirement age from 62 to 64, thereby securing the support of the PS.

This episode shows how parliamentary opposition can turn mass discontent into an object of political bargaining. Here the PS uses workers’ discontent to strengthen its own position within the system of parliamentary power. Refusing to back a no-confidence vote in exchange for a temporary suspension of pension reform is a textbook example of how social conflict is turned into a means of preserving governmental stability. Workers are invited to consider a partial postponement a sufficient result of struggle, even though the very state policy that generated the conflict remains intact. For the politics of the “lesser evil”, this is a characteristic mechanism: a limited concession is presented as sufficient grounds for supporting a government that continues to pursue the general policy of capital.

A government living from vote to vote on the ballots of “socialists” and paying for them with a postponement of pension reform resembles a tightrope walker. Its independence is illusory: it is generated not by strength, but by the fact that class and parliamentary forces mutually block one another. Yet this is the independence of a cabinet, not of the state. Cabinets change, while the budget, the debt, and the multi-year military programming law remain, and the state apparatus continues to organise the general conditions of accumulation. Each new deal narrows the space for the next: concessions bought once have to be bought again.

Article 49.3 is not a violation of the constitution: it was written in 1958 precisely for governments that need to push decisions through without a reliable majority. Therefore, its use does not abolish “democracy”: it shows that the mechanisms of intra-bourgeois consensus have stopped working and what was provided for such an eventuality has been put into play. There is only one condition: the cabinet remains in power as long as no majority assembles against it. For the 2027 budget, observers see three possible outcomes: passage of the law by parliament, use of Article 49.3, or – if the budget is not adopted by year’s end – a special law extending the 2026 budget or a budget enacted by ordinance.

Fiscal discipline, meanwhile, is presented as a demand of reality itself. The deficit must be reduced, public debt stabilised, spending reviewed. Economic constraints exist, but it does not follow that there is only one way to distribute their consequences. Under the draft 2027 budget, the deficit – standing at around 5.4 % of GDP in the current year – is to be reduced to 5 %.

Yet a budget is not a law of the ancestors carved in stone. It is the result of political decisions on taxes, spending, subsidies, military orders, debt service, and the distribution of public resources.

When it comes to schools, universities, healthcare, or social support, a multitude of reasons suddenly appear why the state supposedly cannot afford what is “desirable”. When it comes to military spending, bailing out financial institutions, or supporting strategic industries, the room to find funds frequently expands. The capitalist economy possesses a remarkable ability to discover reserves precisely where they are needed to preserve its key mechanisms. The figures cited earlier make this plain: in the draft 2027 budget, military spending grows by €6.4 billion, education by €1.2 billion.

Marx noted that every article of a constitution can contain its own opposite: a formally proclaimed freedom receives practical restrictions, and a guaranteed right receives conditions under which its exercise becomes impossible. Modern French constitutional procedure is no exception. A government can invoke parliamentary legitimacy while simultaneously using a mechanism that allows a budget decision to be pushed through without an ordinary vote on its content. The legal form is preserved, yet political conflict reveals the limits of representation: the working class can protest against the allocation of spending, but the final decision remains in the hands of the state apparatus and parliamentary forces operating within the existing order.

In the autumn of 2026, high school student protests that began in mid-September grew into a nationwide movement. By October 1, according to the Ministry of the Interior, actions had affected 1,027 of roughly 3,700 high schools, 222 of which were blockaded; the next day, more than 400 educational institutions closed. On October 6, according to the Ministry of the Interior’s estimate, 256,000 people took to the streets; organisers put the number of participants at 450,000. This discrepancy cannot be treated as an exact measurement of the balance of forces: counting methodologies differ. Yet it is telling in itself. The scale of the movement becomes an object of political representation: state statistics and organisers’ estimates yield two different pictures of mass mobilisation. High schoolers were joined by university students, parents, teachers, and workers taking part in union actions. Participants protested against underfunding in education, deteriorating study conditions (classes of 40 or more students, teacher shortages, dilapidated buildings), and the rising cost of living. Student and union organisations are demanding €10 billion in additional funding for education, permanent contracts for teachers, and 250,000 additional university places. Authorities announced support measures, including funding for school and campus repairs, extra places in higher education, and indexation of student grants. Protesters declared these concessions insufficient. On October 7, Lecornu announced that the 2027 education budget provides for a €1.2 billion funding increase. Yet that figure was already contained in the draft budget presented on October 1 and was not a new decision taken in response to the protests. The government presented a previously planned spending increase as part of its response to youth demands, even though the same budget bill simultaneously provided for cutting 1,588 teaching posts, including 836 posts in public secondary education. Funding for the secondary education programme grew by only 0.7%. The ministry explained the cuts by demographic decline and claimed that under a strictly proportional calculation 9,600 posts could have been cut. But that comparison does not alter the main point: the state announces an increase in total expenditure while simultaneously cutting the number of workers directly delivering the educational process.

Therefore, the announced €1.2 billion cannot be considered a concession won by the protest. It is a previously planned budget increase that the government used to create the impression that it was already responding to the movement’s demands. The distinction is fundamental: if the repetition of an earlier budget promise is mistaken for the result of mass struggle, the state’s repackaging of its own plans will be passed off as a victory for the protesters. The real outcome must be measured not by the solemnity of a government statement or by a single headline sum, but by changes in learning conditions, staffing levels, access to education, and the actual fulfilment of the movement’s demands.

The state can provide funds for education while simultaneously continuing fiscal consolidation, tightening pressure on other social spending, or preserving the general conditions of capital accumulation. It can negotiate with protesters and use police force against demonstrations. There is no logical contradiction in this: concessions and coercion are different means of managing class conflicts.

Coercion, meanwhile, is no stage prop. On October 6, 2026 alone, according to the Ministry of the Interior, 488 people were detained and 43 members of the forces of bourgeois order were injured. By that day, according to the government, more than 6,500 people – mostly minors – had been detained since the start of the movement, with 215 students and 715 police officers injured. Engels defined the state through special bodies of armed men, prisons, and coercive institutions; in the final analysis, that is precisely what it is, and those men can be counted. Concessions and the baton issue from one and the same apparatus.

Therefore, one should neither devalue the results achieved nor turn them into proof of the viability of reformism. For participants in the movement, additional university places or improved study conditions have quite concrete meaning. Yet it does not follow that the successive accumulation of such concessions can restore the former model of social compromise.

However, the expansion of social conflict does not happen automatically. Discontent among students, workers in particular sectors, and other groups of working people may share common causes, but that does not yet mean it coalesces into a unified movement. For that, practical links between different centres of struggle, mutual support, the formulation of common demands, and an understanding of which social relations generate separate conflicts are required. It is precisely in this process that political struggle goes beyond particular interests and acquires class content. The task is not to dissolve one movement into another, but to overcome fragmentation and create conditions for joint action.

High school and university students formed the core of the movement, yet it did not remain confined to educational institutions. Education unions backed the protesters and called on workers to strike on October 8 and 13; the CGT Education Federation and other organisations demanded general assemblies at workplaces to discuss continuing the struggle. According to the CGT, more than a million participants in total took part in the demonstrations of October 6, 8, and 13. On October 17, the CGT supported an even broader mobilisation for higher wages and pensions, lower prices, and the defence of public services. Thus, the movement gained the support and participation of workers, and its demands began to intersect with broader social conflicts. Yet this still does not mean that the working class acted as a single independent political force: union calls and the participation of particular categories of workers do not in themselves replace sustained coordination of struggle at enterprises and across sectors.

What remains after this mobilisation ends? Do independent forms of organisation survive? Do lasting ties emerge in the course of struggle between separate groups and sectors? Does workers’ capacity to act together without handing leadership of the movement to parliamentary representatives and professional intermediaries develop? Do the strength and membership of the world communist party grow? The answer to these questions will be the real assessment of the current student movement.

Here, however, we must also raise the question of those organisations already present in the movement and claiming to express its interests. Their participation does not in itself indicate the emergence of a revolutionary perspective. The PCF, the PS, and La France insoumise are political organisations seeking to gain influence over mass discontent and channel it into their own state and electoral strategies. Their differences do not abolish their common class limit: none of them sets as its practical task the destruction of the bourgeois state and the establishment of the independent political power of the working class.

Why, then, do these organisations participate in the protests? Their participation does not mean they seek to push the protests to a revolutionary collision with the state. They need to retain influence over the protesters, not cede the initiative to other forces, preserve their organisational base, and present themselves as recognised intermediaries between discontent from below and political institutions. Parliamentary parties gain the opportunity to convert mass mobilisation into electoral capital; the union leadership confirms its status as a negotiator capable both of organising pressure and of bringing it to a halt. Herein lies the mechanism of the political appropriation of a movement: its participants take to the streets for their own demands, while organisations claiming leadership strive to determine in what forms, up to what limits, and for what outcome this struggle will continue.

A revolutionary perspective can emerge not from the reorientation of these apparatuses, but from the independent action of the movement’s participants themselves, from their uniting with workers, creating elected and accountable organs of struggle, and stepping beyond parliamentary mediation. If the movement ends by handing demands over to politicians and waiting for the next favourable decision, its possibilities remain confined within the existing order. If, however, the struggle strengthens independent organisation and the capacity of working people to collectively determine their own actions, it can acquire a broader class significance.

This approach does not require declaring the French protests in advance to be either tragedy or farce. Their significance is determined by the development of the struggle itself. Yet this approach rules out the reformist illusion that it suffices to win one more concession to reopen the path toward the gradual transformation of capitalism. Farce arises when a class movement contents itself with borrowed costumes and fails to create its own forms of organisation and its own programme.

The task of communists is neither to harness the movement nor to command it from behind its back, yet also not to leave it at the mercy of parliamentary intermediaries. It is necessary to foster the development of independent organisation, connect individual demands with general conditions of life and labour, and pose the question of who disposes of social resources and makes decisions. Communists are an integral part of the real movement of the working class, distinguished from all other participants by the greatest consistency and clarity of programme.

That is precisely why the French example cannot be reduced to a simple choice between the government and its parliamentary opponents. The differences between the presidential camp, right-wing nationalists, and the parliamentary left carry political significance, yet none of these camps becomes an exponent of the independent interests of the working class merely by virtue of being in opposition. The government may represent the interests of finance capital; the right-wing opposition may combine nationalist mobilisation with promises to protect particular groups; the parliamentary left may channel discontent toward restoring a reformist compromise. Their programmes differ, yet the dispute over who should manage the state does not call into question the rule of capital itself.

## VI. From Protest to Organisation

The article began with the question of tragedy and farce. The answer to it is practical: borrowed costumes are not needed. What is needed is what the French workers’ movement lacked in May 1968: a revolutionary party and a programme capable of connecting a mass strike with the struggle for political power. Back then, sufficiently powerful workers’ organisations, trade unions, parties, and numerous organs of strike struggle existed. Yet their strength was not united by an independent revolutionary strategy. The union and party apparatuses subordinated the mass movement to a politics of deals and parliamentary manoeuvring, while the revolutionary alternative could not become the leading force of the struggle. What does this mean for communists today?

_First – linking the centres of struggle._ High school and university committees, strike committees at enterprises, and union sections must not exist in parallel worlds. General assemblies, delegates with recallable mandates, joint leaflets, and mutual assistance through strike funds are needed. It is precisely through such organs that workers learn to elect representatives and control them – that is, to do what an electorate does not do.

_Second – demands that connect the particular with the general._ Schools cannot be defended without raising the question of where public money goes. A slogan such as “money for schools, wages, and hospitals, not for armaments and banks” is more concrete than general appeals for justice and carries the protest beyond the bounds of a single sector to the question of political power and control over the results of socialised labour.

_Third – party press and organisation._ A newspaper must be not only a collective propagandist and agitator, but also a collective organiser of the struggle. Through it, through leaflets, speeches, and direct work among working people, the party links scattered actions into a single movement, generalises the experience of struggle, exposes the politics of intermediaries, and explains whom the compromises they propose serve. It helps workers distinguish a temporary concession from a genuine conquest, an isolated success from a shift in the balance of class forces. The party does not merely accompany every campaign with agitation, but uses it to strengthen its own organisation, train new cadres, and expand its influence within the working class. Therefore, growth in the party’s membership, political maturity, and organised strength is one of the most important criteria for the success of any campaign, alongside the immediate results of the struggle.

_Fourth – international organisation of the struggle._ Capital has long operated across national borders: relocating production, allocating investment, organising supply chains, and exploiting competition among workers of different countries. Workers’ resistance remaining within the confines of nation-states is forced to fight an adversary that possesses international means of pressure and manoeuvre. Therefore, ties among workers in Germany, Italy, Spain, France, and other countries must become not a symbol of solidarity, but a practical element of organisation: exchanging information, formulating joint demands, coordinating strikes, and resisting the relocation of production and the deterioration of working conditions. Proletarian internationalism begins not with a declaration of unity, but with organising joint action against a capital that has long since ceased to recognise national borders.

_Fifth – a class position on imperialist war._ The answer to the question of who is fighting and for what was given long ago: in a war between imperialist powers, the workers of both sides fight for alien interests. Therefore, communists refuse to defend national interests – to support “their own” state. The task, as Lenin posed it, is turning the imperialist war into a civil war.

_Sixth – clarity on the ultimate goal._ We tell workers directly: no change of shell will change power, and the power of the working class means smashing the bourgeois state apparatus and establishing the dictatorship of the proletariat in a form analogous in content to the Commune and the Soviets. Everything that lies between today’s strike and this goal consists of tasks of organisation.

And finally. The social revolution, Marx wrote, must draw its poetry from the future. Whoever contents himself with the wreckage of the past is doomed to farce. Yet exposing old political shells changes nothing by itself either. Criticism acquires revolutionary content only at the moment it becomes part of the practical work of building new forms of class organisation. The costumes of the past may recall earlier battles, but they cannot replace organisation, a programme, and the independent action of the working class. The main test of any movement lies in what remains after it: only the memory of demonstrations and a list of promised concessions, or an organisation capable of continuing the struggle the next day, uniting new forces, and posing the question of political power.
